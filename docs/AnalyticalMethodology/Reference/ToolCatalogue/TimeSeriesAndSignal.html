    Time series and signal tool catalogue

    Jube.Service/Agent/AdvancedAnalyticsTools/TimeSeriesAndSignal is one of the ten semantic tool groups that the AdvancedAnalytics agent layer exposes directly to the LLM by name: 111 methods, each decorated [AdvancedAnalyticsTool("ToolName")], grouped into nine tool classes covering Markov chains and hidden Markov models, sequence and pattern analysis, series anomaly and change detection, dependence and seasonality tests, forecasting, series transforms and filters, spectral and signal analysis, and transaction-timing statistics. None of these 111 names appears anywhere else under docs/, which means Jadek, the validator agent that fact-checks every AskJooby reply against a RAG-indexed corpus built from this documentation tree, cannot confirm a claim about any of them without it. This page covers every tool. Two supporting files in the same directory, MarkovMath.cs (matrix and stationary-distribution helpers) and TimeSeriesGuard.cs (input validation and shared statistics), are not tools themselves and are not catalogued here.

    Unless stated otherwise, every tool in this group is stateless: it takes plain arrays (double[], string[], int[], jagged double[][]) and returns a typed result record, with no reference to a model, a payload or engine state. Numeric inputs are validated by TimeSeriesGuard before use, so the error a caller sees for a malformed array is a descriptive ArgumentException naming the parameter, not an unhandled exception.

    HiddenMarkovTools.cs

    Eleven tools for discrete- and Gaussian-emission hidden Markov models (HMMs): decoding, likelihood, posterior state probabilities, next-value prediction, and Baum-Welch training. A discrete HMM models a sequence of categorical symbols; a Gaussian HMM models a numeric series as a regime-switching normal distribution. All eleven share a model shape of transitions (a square, row-stochastic matrix), initial (a probability vector) and, for discrete models, emissions (a row-stochastic matrix of symbol probabilities per state) or, for Gaussian models, means and standardDeviations per state. Every probability row must sum to 1 within 1e-6, every standard deviation must be above 0, and all eleven are limited to 12 hidden states and 200,000 observations; violating any of these throws an ArgumentException naming the offending row or dimension.

    HiddenMarkovDiscreteDecode runs the Viterbi algorithm on a discrete HMM to find the single most likely sequence of hidden states behind an observed symbol sequence (symbol codes from 0 to the emission column count minus 1). It returns StatePath (zero-based decoded states) and LogLikelihoodOfPath, the natural-log probability of that path together with the observations. Use it to label each step of a behaviour sequence with a latent regime, for example normal versus compromised, under a model supplied directly or produced by HiddenMarkovDiscreteTrain.

    HiddenMarkovDiscreteLogLikelihood computes the log-likelihood of an observed discrete sequence under a given model by the forward algorithm, summing over all hidden paths rather than picking the best one. It returns LogLikelihood, AverageLogLikelihoodPerObservation (for comparing sequences of different lengths), and StepLogProbabilities with MostSurprisingIndex locating the single most improbable step; a symbol the model gives zero probability produces negative infinity at that step.

    HiddenMarkovDiscretePosterior runs the forward-backward algorithm to give, for every step, the full posterior probability of each hidden state given the whole sequence (past and future), rather than one decoded path. It returns StateProbabilities[t][i] (each row summing to 1), MostLikelyStates[t] (which can differ from the Viterbi path), and LogLikelihood. Use it when the confidence in each step’s state assignment matters more than a single hard labelling.

    HiddenMarkovDiscreteNextSymbolDistribution predicts the next symbol in a sequence under a discrete HMM: it filters the current belief about the hidden state from everything observed so far, pushes that belief one step through the transition matrix, and projects it through the emission matrix. It returns FilteredStateProbabilities, NextStateProbabilities, NextSymbolProbabilities (summing to 1) and MostLikelyNextSymbol, which supports next-action prediction or checking whether an actual next event was expected.

    HiddenMarkovGaussianDecode is the Gaussian-emission counterpart of HiddenMarkovDiscreteDecode: Viterbi decoding of the most likely sequence of hidden regimes behind a numeric series (for example daily volume labelled quiet, normal or busy), under a model supplied directly or trained with HiddenMarkovGaussianTrain. It returns StatePath and LogLikelihoodOfPath (a log density, so it can be positive since this is a density rather than a probability mass).

    HiddenMarkovGaussianLogLikelihood computes the forward-algorithm log-likelihood of a numeric series under a Gaussian HMM, returning LogLikelihood, AverageLogLikelihoodPerObservation, StepLogProbabilities and MostSurprisingIndex, to judge model fit, compare regime counts, or flag a series a normal-behaviour model finds unlikely.

    HiddenMarkovGaussianPosterior is the Gaussian counterpart of HiddenMarkovDiscretePosterior: smoothed posterior regime probabilities at every point of a numeric series from the forward-backward algorithm, returning StateProbabilities, MostLikelyStates and LogLikelihood, used to see how confident a regime assignment is rather than only its most likely value.

    HiddenMarkovGaussianNextValueDistribution gives a one-step-ahead forecast of a numeric series under a Gaussian HMM: ExpectedNextValue (the mixture mean across regimes weighted by their filtered and transitioned probabilities) and NextValueStandardDeviation (the mixture standard deviation, combining within-regime spread and regime uncertainty), alongside FilteredStateProbabilities and NextStateProbabilities. A new observation can be standardised by subtracting ExpectedNextValue and dividing by NextValueStandardDeviation before comparing it with a threshold.

    HiddenMarkovDiscreteTrain fits a discrete HMM to example symbol sequences with Baum-Welch (expectation-maximisation), discovering latent regimes behind categorical event streams with no labels, for example channel or merchant-category sequences. Parameters: states (1 to 12), symbols (0, the default, uses the largest code seen plus 1), restarts (default 3, 1 to 20), maxIterations (default 100, 1 to 1000), tolerance (default 1e-6) and seed (default 1, for reproducibility). Training is repeated restarts times from different random starting probabilities (seeded, so reproducible) because Baum-Welch finds only a local optimum, and the run with the highest log-likelihood is returned, with fitted probabilities floored away from exactly zero. The result’s Transitions, Emissions and Initial carry an arbitrary hidden-state numbering; LogLikelihood, Aic and Bic measure fit. It requires at least 2 observations per hidden state in total and at least 2 distinct symbol codes.

    HiddenMarkovGaussianTrain is the Gaussian counterpart: it fits a regime-switching normal model to one or more numeric series by Baum-Welch, learning a mean, standard deviation, persistence and switching probability for each regime. Means are seeded by spreading them across the data quantiles with reproducible jitter after the first restart; minimumStandardDeviationFraction (default 0.001, strictly between 0 and 1) floors each regime’s fitted standard deviation as a fraction of the pooled standard deviation, preventing a regime from collapsing onto a single point. Regimes in the result are ordered by increasing mean. It throws if every training value is identical (no variation to separate regimes), and otherwise shares the restarts, iteration limits and 12-state, 200,000-observation caps of the discrete trainer.

    HiddenMarkovGaussianSelectStateCount answers “how many regimes does this series need?” before calling HiddenMarkovGaussianTrain: it fits models with 1 up to maxStates (default 4) regimes and compares them by AIC and BIC (lower is better; BIC penalises complexity more). Candidates lists each tried state count’s log-likelihood, AIC, BIC and parameter count; BestStatesByBic and BestStatesByAic name the winners (usually BIC favours the simpler model) and BestModel is the fitted model at the BIC winner. A single regime is fitted as the plain normal distribution rather than a one-state HMM.

    MarkovChainTools.cs

    Seven tools for first-order Markov chains over labelled states: estimating a chain from an observed sequence, and analysing a chain whose transition matrix is already known (stationary behaviour, multi-step projection, mean first-passage times, and absorption). State labels are arbitrary strings; chains supplied as a matrix are capped at 500 states via MarkovMath.

    MarkovChainTransitionMatrix estimates a first-order Markov chain from an observed sequence of state labels in time order (for example account status changes, transaction channels or risk bands), and summarises its behaviour in one call. States lists the distinct labels in alphabetical order, indexing every other array; TransitionCounts and TransitionProbabilities (counts plus smoothing, default 0, divided by the row total) describe the chain; StationaryDistribution is the long-run time share per state (StationaryIsUnique is false when several separate recurrent classes exist); ExpectedReturnSteps is 1 divided by that share; MostLikelyNextState and EntropyRateBits summarise predictability; AbsorbingStates lists states the chain never leaves; and with no smoothing, a state that only appears at the end of the sequence (no observed exit) is treated as self-absorbing and listed separately in StatesWithoutOutgoingTransitions. Needs at least 2 states in the sequence, and at most 12 distinct states (MarkovMath.MaximumStates) — group rare states first if the raw alphabet is larger.

    MarkovChainStationaryDistribution computes the long-run distribution of a chain whose transition matrix is already known (each row summing to 1), rather than estimated from data. IsUnique is true only when the chain has a single recurrent class, in which case the distribution does not depend on the starting state; with several recurrent classes the result is an average from a uniform start, flagged in Note. It also reports RecurrentClasses, TransientStates, AbsorbingStates and ExpectedReturnSteps (1 / stationary probability). Limited to 500 states.

    MarkovChainMultiStepProbabilities projects the state distribution forward a given number of steps (0 to 1,000,000) from either startState (a zero-based index) or startDistribution (a probability vector) — exactly one of the two, not both. StepMatrix is the transition matrix raised to the power steps (computed by binary exponentiation, so large step counts are cheap), and Distribution is the projected state probabilities. Use it to project where an account or case is likely to be in 3 or 12 periods from now.

    MarkovChainSequenceSurprise scores how unusual a new (testSequence) sequence of states is against a Markov chain learned from a reference (trainingSequence), with additive smoothing (default 1, must be above 0 so unseen moves are not impossible) over the union of states seen in either sequence. SurprisalBits[t] is minus log2 of the probability of each observed transition (0 bits certain, 1 bit a coin flip); AverageBitsPerTransition and Perplexity (2 to that power) summarise the whole test sequence; MostSurprisingTransition and UnseenTransitions (moves never seen in training) round it out. Use it to flag behaviour that departs from a learned pattern of state-to-state movement.

    MarkovChainMeanFirstPassageTimes computes, for a chain with a known transition matrix, the expected number of steps to first reach each target state from each starting state. MeanFirstPassageSteps[i][j] is that expectation; the diagonal [j][j] is the mean recurrence time, equal to 1 / stationary probability for an irreducible chain. An entry is infinity when the target state cannot always be reached from the source. Answers questions like “on average how many periods until an active account becomes dormant?” Limited to 500 states.

    MarkovChainAbsorptionAnalysis analyses an absorbing chain — one that ends in one of several final states, each with probability 1 of staying put, for example a case closed as confirmed, dismissed or escalated. It returns ExpectedStepsToAbsorption per transient state, AbsorptionProbabilities[i][k] (the chance of ending in the k-th absorbing state from the i-th transient one, each row summing to 1) and ExpectedVisits (the fundamental matrix). It throws if no state is absorbing (use MarkovChainStationaryDistribution instead for a chain that never ends) or if a recurrent class is not a single absorbing state. Limited to 500 states.

    MarkovDependenceTest is a chi-square test of whether the next state in a sequence depends on the current one at all — whether a Markov model has anything to add over treating states as independent draws. It forms a current-state-by-next-state contingency table from the sequence and tests it for independence; a small PValue (with Dependent true) means real state-to-state structure exists, and CramersV (0 to 1) measures its strength. Note warns when many expected cell counts fall below 5, where the chi-square approximation becomes unreliable. Needs at least 11 state labels with at least 2 distinct current and next states.

    SequencePatternTools.cs

    Nine tools for categorical and numeric sequence pattern analysis: n-gram frequency, entropy and complexity measures, run-length encoding, dimensionality-reducing approximations (PAA and SAX), and two sequence-alignment distances (DTW and Levenshtein edit distance). Symbol sequences are compared exactly (ordinal, case-sensitive) throughout.

    NGramFrequencyTable counts the most frequent runs of n (default 2) consecutive symbols in an ordered categorical sequence, such as transaction types, channels or event codes, to find recurring behavioural patterns (for example a typical three-step path before a chargeback). NGrams[i], Counts[i] and Shares[i] describe the topK (default 20) most frequent patterns, ties broken alphabetically; DistinctNGrams and TotalNGrams (length minus n plus 1) describe the whole table. Needs at least n symbols.

    SymbolSequenceEntropyProfile computes the Shannon entropy profile of a symbol sequence: how unpredictable it is on its own (BlockEntropiesBits, entropy of blocks of k symbols) and once earlier symbols are known (ConditionalEntropiesBits, which falls toward 0 as the sequence becomes more predictable from its history). NormalisedFirstOrderEntropy divides entropy by log2 of the alphabet size (NaN for a one-symbol alphabet) and Redundancy is 1 minus it. Use it to separate rigid, scripted or automated behaviour (low entropy) from varied human behaviour. Estimates from short sequences are biased low, especially at higher orders; maxOrder (default 3) is reduced automatically to fit the sequence length.

    LempelZivSequenceComplexity computes the Lempel-Ziv (1976) complexity of a symbol sequence — the number of distinct new phrases encountered when parsing left to right — as a model-free, compression-based measure of patternedness: repetitive sequences score low, irregular ones high (the classic example 0001101001000101 scores PhraseCount 6). NormalisedComplexity is PhraseCount x log(n) / n in a logarithm base equal to the alphabet size (NaN for a single-symbol alphabet), close to 1 for random sequences and near 0 for highly repetitive ones. Needs at least 2 symbols; reliable normalised values need a few dozen or more.

    RunLengthEncodeSequence run-length encodes a sequence, collapsing each unbroken stretch of the same symbol into one run, to measure how long a customer stays in one status or repeats an action. It returns Symbols, Lengths and StartIndices per run plus RunCount, LongestRun and MeanRunLength. Needs at least 1 symbol.

    ThresholdRunLengths finds unbroken streaks of numeric values that satisfy a threshold condition — Above, AtOrAbove, Below or AtOrBelow (default Above) — such as consecutive days with volume above a limit, to measure persistence of an alert condition. StartIndices and Lengths describe each streak with RunCount, LongestRun, MeanRunLength and ShareOfPointsInRuns; missing (NaN) values never satisfy the condition and break a streak. Returns zeros when no value qualifies.

    PiecewiseAggregateApproximation (PAA) shrinks a series to a fixed number of segments (1 to the series length) by averaging equal-width pieces, points straddling a segment boundary split proportionally, giving a fixed-length shape summary regardless of input length. For [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6] with 3 segments the result is [1.5, 3.5, 5.5]. Use it to compare series of different lengths, build fixed-size features, or as the first step of SAX.

    SymbolicAggregateApproximation (SAX) turns a numeric series into a short word of letters: the series is optionally standardised to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (standardise, default true), reduced to segments PAA averages, and each average mapped to a letter using breakpoints that split a standard normal distribution into alphabetSize (2 to 20, default 4) equally likely bins. Word, Symbols, Paa, Breakpoints, Mean and StandardDeviation are all returned. A constant series cannot be standardised and is mapped to the middle letters. Use it to compare series shapes, feed NGramFrequencyTable, or precede other sequence tools.

    DynamicTimeWarpingDistance (DTW) finds the smallest total cost of aligning two numeric series when either may be stretched or compressed in time, so shapes that are shifted or run at different speeds still match. Point cost is absolute (default) or squared (squaredCost) difference; bandRadius (default -1, meaning unrestricted) limits how far an alignment may drift from the diagonal, widened automatically to at least the difference in the two lengths. Returns Distance, NormalisedDistance (divided by path length) and Path (the aligned index pairs). Identical series give 0. The work scales with the product of the two lengths, capped at 25 million cells — reduce the series first (for example with PiecewiseAggregateApproximation) if that is exceeded.

    SymbolSequenceEditDistance computes the Levenshtein edit distance between two ordered symbol sequences: the fewest single-symbol insertions, deletions and substitutions to turn one into the other, for comparing behavioural paths or customer-journey steps. Distance is the raw count, NormalisedDistance divides by the longer sequence’s length (0 identical, 1 completely different) and Similarity is 1 minus that. Either sequence may be empty; both empty give distance 0 and similarity 1.

    SeriesAnomalyTools.cs

    Eight tools for detecting change, drift and anomalies in a numeric series: four control-chart style monitors (CUSUM, Page-Hinkley, EWMA, Shewhart), a general-purpose spike detector, a level-shift scanner, a binary-segmentation change-point finder, and drawdown analysis. The control-chart tools share a Baseline helper: when targetMean or standardDeviation is left NaN (the default for both), they are estimated from the first baselineCount values (0 default means the first half of the series, up to 30 points), and the call throws if the resulting baseline standard deviation is 0 or undefined (a constant or too-short baseline).

    CusumChangeDetection is a two-sided CUSUM control chart that detects a sustained shift in a series’ mean, up or down, earlier than a single-point threshold would. Each value contributes its deviation from the target minus a slack allowance kSigma (default 0.5) standard deviations to a running sum floored at 0; an alarm fires when a sum exceeds hSigma (default 5) standard deviations, after which both sums reset. UpperSums and LowerSums are reported per position, and AlarmIndices come with AlarmDirections (Up/Down) and EstimatedChangeStarts (where the alarming sum last left 0). Default settings detect a one-sigma shift within roughly 10 points.

    PageHinkleyDriftDetection is the Page-Hinkley test for drift, suited to online monitoring with no fixed baseline: it compares each value with the running average of everything since the last alarm. delta (default 0) is the magnitude of change tolerated without alarm; lambda (default 5, in series units) is the alarm threshold. IncreaseStatistic and DecreaseStatistic accumulate rise and fall evidence; an alarm in either direction resets the running mean and both accumulators. AlarmIndices and AlarmDirections report the hits.

    EwmaControlChart smooths a series with weight lambda (default 0.2, above 0 and at most 1) on the newest value and alarms when the smoothed value leaves control limits of targetMean plus or minus limitWidth (default 3) standard errors, catching small persistent shifts that a Shewhart chart misses while still reacting to large ones. Ewma, the time-varying UpperLimits/LowerLimits (which widen to a steady width over the first few points) and AlarmIndices are all returned. Typical settings are lambda 0.05 to 0.25.

    ShewhartControlChartAlarms applies the four standard Western Electric run rules to a series standardised against a target and standard deviation (ZScores): Rule 1, a point beyond 3 sigma; Rule 2, 2 of 3 consecutive points beyond 2 sigma on the same side; Rule 3, 4 of 5 beyond 1 sigma on the same side; Rule 4, 8 consecutive points on one side. Each rule’s hits are reported in its own array plus a unioned, ordered AlarmIndices.

    SeriesSpikeDetection flags spikes and dips with a choice of method (default RollingRobustZScore): RollingZScore (mean/standard deviation of the preceding window, default 30), RollingRobustZScore (median and 1.4826 x median absolute deviation of the preceding window, resistant to outliers distorting their own baseline), GlobalRobustZScore (the whole series’ median and deviation) or InterquartileFence (distance beyond Q1/Q3 in interquartile ranges; 1.5 is Tukey’s fence, 3 is “far out”). Scores has one entry per value (NaN where no baseline exists yet, such as the first window); SpikeIndices and SpikeValues list positions whose absolute score exceeds threshold (default 3.5). A zero-spread baseline scores any different value as infinite.

    LevelShiftScan locates level shifts — a step up or down that persists, as distinct from a one-off spike — by comparing the mean of the window (default 10) before each point with the mean of the window starting there, via a Welch t-type score. Scores[t] is defined only where a full window exists on both sides; ShiftIndices (the strongest points exceeding threshold, default 5, no two closer than one window apart) come with ShiftSizes and ShiftScores. ShiftIndices[i] marks the first observation at the new level. Needs at least twice the window.

    MeanShiftChangePoints splits a series into segments of roughly constant mean by binary segmentation on sum of squared errors, without choosing a window in advance. The noise level is estimated robustly from the median absolute first difference; a split survives only if it beats a BIC-style penalty scaled by penaltyMultiplier (default 1; above 1 is more conservative) and both resulting parts have at least minSegmentLength (default 5) points. Splits are added greedily, largest gain first, up to maxChangePoints (default 5). ChangePointIndices with Gains and Segments (start, end, size, mean of each piece) describe the result; an empty change-point list means no significant shift was found.

    DrawdownAnalysis measures, for a level series such as an account balance or cumulative total, how far it falls below its previous high-water mark and for how long. Drawdowns[t] is the level minus the running maximum up to t (0 at a new high); MaximumDrawdown (in level units) and MaximumDrawdownFraction (of the peak it fell from, NaN if that peak is 0 or negative) locate the worst fall via PeakIndex and TroughIndex; RecoveryIndex is the first later point back at that peak level (-1 if it never recovered); LongestUnderwaterSteps and CurrentUnderwaterSteps (the run in progress at the series end) measure duration.

    SeriesDependenceTools.cs

    Fourteen tools for testing and measuring dependence, randomness, trend and seasonality in a series: the correlogram family (ACF, PACF, cross-correlation, Ljung-Box, Box-Pierce, Durbin-Watson), two non-parametric randomness tests (turning points, median runs), a trend test (Mann-Kendall), classical seasonal decomposition and its derived seasonality-strength and period-estimation tools, and two regression-based lag tests (ARCH effects, Granger causality).

    AutocorrelationFunction (ACF) computes the sample autocorrelation at lags 0 to maxLag (default 20, reduced to series length minus 1 if longer): Values[0] is always 1. WhiteNoiseBound is the plain significance band for pure noise; BartlettBounds is a wider, lag-specific band that allows for correlation already seen at shorter lags; SignificantLags lists lags beyond the plain bound. A constant series returns NaN above lag 0. Use it to see whether a value depends on earlier ones, to spot repeating cycles (a peak at lag 7 in daily data is a weekly pattern), or to check that residuals are white noise.

    PartialAutocorrelationFunction (PACF) computes, via the Durbin-Levinson recursion from the ACF, the correlation at lag k after removing the effect of the shorter lags in between — used to choose the order of an autoregressive model, since an AR(p) process’s PACF cuts off to about zero after lag p while its ACF tails off gradually. Values start at lag 1 (unlike AutocorrelationFunction, which includes lag 0); needs at least 4 finite values and returns NaN for a constant series.

    CrossCorrelationFunction computes the correlation between two equal-length series at lags from -maxLag to +maxLag (default 10), to find whether one leads the other and by how many steps, for example card-present spend leading online spend. A positive lag’s large correlation means x leads y; a negative lag’s means y leads x. BestLag/BestCorrelation report the strongest absolute match; Bound and SignificantLags flag significance. A constant series on either side returns NaN everywhere.

    LjungBoxAutocorrelationTest is the standard portmanteau test that a series (or a model’s residuals) has no autocorrelation up to lags (default 10): Statistic (Q) follows chi-square with DegreesOfFreedom = lags - modelDegreesOfFreedom (set the latter, default 0, to the number of fitted ARMA parameters when testing residuals). A small PValue means autocorrelation is present. StatisticByLag/PValueByLag give the running test at every lag. Requires lags smaller than the series length; prefer this over BoxPierceAutocorrelationTest unless the exact older statistic is specifically needed.

    BoxPierceAutocorrelationTest is the original, less accurate form of the same portmanteau test (Q = n x sum of squared autocorrelations), kept for when that exact statistic, rather than the more accurate Ljung-Box correction, is required. Same parameters and result shape as LjungBoxAutocorrelationTest.

    DurbinWatsonResidualAutocorrelation computes the classic Durbin-Watson statistic on an ordered series of model residuals (sum of squared successive differences over sum of squared values, with residuals not centred first, per the textbook definition): near 2 means no first-order autocorrelation, well below 2 means positive autocorrelation, well above 2 (toward 4) means negative. ImpliedFirstOrderAutocorrelation is approximately 1 - Statistic/2; Reading labels the result using cut-offs of 1.5 and 2.5. Returns NaN if every residual is zero.

    TurningPointRandomnessTest is a distribution-free check that a series (or residuals) is random: it counts local peaks and troughs and compares the count with the number expected under independence (2(n-2)/3, variance (16n-29)/90). ZScore and the normal-approximation PValue flag departure; too few turning points means smooth persistence or trend, too many means alternating (oscillating) behaviour. Ties do not count as turning points; needs at least 4 values, with the normal approximation most reliable from about 10 upward.

    MedianRunsRandomnessTest is the Wald-Wolfowitz runs test on the sequence of values above and below the series median: a large negative ZScore means clustering or trend, a large positive one means alternation. Values equal to the median are ignored, and the call throws if every remaining value falls on only one side (a constant or near-constant series).

    MannKendallTrendTest is the non-parametric Mann-Kendall test for a monotonic trend, paired with the robust Theil-Sen slope. S is the sum of signs of all later-minus-earlier pairwise differences; ZScore uses a tie-corrected, continuity-corrected variance; KendallTau rescales S to -1..1; SenSlopePerStep is the median of all pairwise slopes (robust to outliers) and SenIntercept its matching intercept. Trend reads Increasing, Decreasing or NoTrend at the chosen alpha (default 0.05). Assumes independent observations — serial correlation inflates significance. Limited to 3 to 4000 values; for longer series, aggregate first (for example with ResampleEventsToBuckets).

    ClassicalSeasonalDecomposition splits an evenly spaced series into trend (a centred moving average over one full period), a repeating SeasonalIndices/Seasonal pattern, and a remainder, under an Additive (default, value = trend + seasonal + remainder) or Multiplicative (value = trend x seasonal x remainder, requiring every value above 0) model. SeasonalStrength and TrendStrength (0 to 1) summarise how much each component explains. Needs at least two full periods of data; the trend is NaN for the first and last half-period.

    SeasonalityStrengthScore is a thin wrapper around ClassicalSeasonalDecomposition that returns just SeasonalStrength, TrendStrength and a plain-language Reading (Strong/Moderate/Weak or no seasonality at cut-offs of 0.6 and 0.3), to quickly decide whether a seasonal method or adjustment is worthwhile.

    EstimateSeasonalPeriodByAutocorrelation estimates the length of a repeating cycle from the local peaks of the series’ autocorrelation function, for when the period is not known in advance (for example whether activity repeats weekly or every 30 days). The series is optionally de-trended first (detrend, default true, a fitted straight line) so a trend does not mask the cycle. Period is the lag of the strongest significant peak up to maxPeriod (default 0, meaning half the series length); Found is false (Period 0) if no peak clears the white-noise bound. The answer is in observations, not calendar time — resample an irregular event stream to a regular grid first (see ResampleEventsToBuckets).

    EngleArchLmTest is Engle’s Lagrange-multiplier test for ARCH effects (volatility clustering) in residuals or returns: large movements tend to follow large movements regardless of sign. It regresses squared values on lags (default 5) of themselves; Statistic (usable rows times the regression R-squared) follows chi-square under the null of no ARCH effect, and a small PValue means volatility clusters (Significant true). Supply mean-removed residuals; returns NaN if the squared values are constant.

    GrangerCausalityTest tests whether past values of a candidate cause series improve prediction of an effect series beyond the effect series’ own past, via an F test comparing two regressions (with and without the cause’s lags). Statistic, PValue, DegreesOfFreedomNumerator (= lags, default 2) and DegreesOfFreedomDenominator describe the test; Significant flags predictive improvement at alpha (default 0.05). This is predictive precedence, not true causation, and both series should be stationary first (difference a trending series before calling it). Requires equal-length series longer than 3 x lags + 1.

    SeriesForecastingTools.cs

    Ten tools for point forecasting with prediction intervals, model fitting, and forecast evaluation: five benchmark and exponential-smoothing methods, autoregressive (AR) model fitting/order-selection/forecasting, accuracy scoring, and a hold-out comparison that runs several methods and ranks them. All forecasting tools return a SeriesForecastResult shape with Forecast, Lower/Upper prediction bounds at confidenceLevel (default 0.95), Fitted in-sample one-step predictions, ResidualStandardDeviation/sigma, and named ParameterNames/ParameterValues; horizon is capped at 1–10000 steps throughout.

    BaselineSeriesForecast implements five textbook benchmarks that any more elaborate model should beat: Naive (repeat the last value), SeasonalNaive (repeat the value one cycle earlier; needs seasonalPeriod of 2 or more and at least one full cycle), Mean (the overall average), Drift (extend the line joining the first and last values) and MovingAverage (average of the last window, default 3, values). Interval widths use each method’s standard closed-form variance formula (for example Naive widens as sigma x sqrt(h), Drift as sigma x sqrt(h(1+h/n))).

    SimpleExponentialSmoothingForecast produces a flat forecast equal to an exponentially weighted average of the history, for series with no trend or seasonality. alpha (default NaN, estimated by minimising one-step squared error) weights the newest value; the level starts at the first value. Interval width grows as sigma x sqrt(1 + (h-1) x alpha^2).

    HoltLinearTrendForecast tracks a level and a slope that both adapt over time (Holt’s linear trend method), so the forecast continues the recent trend as a straight line, or, with phi (damping factor, default 1) below 1, a trend that flattens out. alpha/beta default to NaN (both estimated together); the level starts at the first value and the slope at the first difference, so a perfectly linear series is reproduced exactly.

    HoltWintersSeasonalForecast adds a repeating seasonal component to Holt’s method, for series with both trend and a cycle (weekly cycles in daily counts, daily cycles in hourly counts). model is Additive (default, constant seasonal swing) or Multiplicative (swing grows with the level; requires every value above 0); alpha/beta/gamma default to NaN (all three estimated jointly). State is initialised from the first two cycles, so at least two full cycles of history are required; a series that is exactly periodic with no trend is reproduced exactly. Multiplicative intervals are approximate (they reuse the additive-form variance).

    LinearTrendForecast extrapolates an ordinary-least-squares straight line against the time index, with exact prediction intervals from the t distribution (n - 2 degrees of freedom). Suited to a steady, roughly linear drift with constant-variance noise; ignores seasonality and assumes independent errors.

    AutoregressiveModelFit fits an AR(order) model — each value a weighted sum of the previous order values plus noise — by YuleWalker (default; from sample autocovariances, always stationary) or LeastSquares (regression on lagged values with an intercept). Coefficients[k-1] multiplies the value k steps earlier; Mean is the implied long-run level (NaN if the coefficients sum to 1, a unit root); Aic/Bic support comparing orders fitted the same way on the same data. For [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] with order 1, the least-squares fit is exact.

    AutoregressiveOrderSelection fits AR orders 0 to maxOrder (default 10) by Yule-Walker and compares them by AIC and BIC, returning BestOrderByAic and BestOrderByBic (order 0 means no dependence on the past; BIC favours smaller models). Requires more than 2 x maxOrder points.

    AutoregressiveForecast forecasts with a fitted AR(order) model: each future value uses the previous order values, falling back to earlier forecasts once the real data run out, so forecasts for a stationary series revert toward the long-run mean. Prediction intervals use the model’s psi (moving-average) weights, so they widen with horizon and then level off. For a trending series, difference it first (DifferenceSeries) or use a trend method instead.

    ForecastAccuracyMetrics scores a set of forecasts against what actually happened: MeanError (signed, positive means under-forecasting), MeanAbsoluteError, MedianAbsoluteError, MeanSquaredError/RootMeanSquaredError (series units), MeanAbsolutePercentageError (a fraction; skips zero actuals, counted in PercentageErrorsSkipped), SymmetricMeanAbsolutePercentageError, and MeanAbsoluteScaledError (mean absolute error scaled by the mean absolute naive — or seasonal-naive, via seasonalPeriod — error on optional trainingValues; below 1 beats the naive method; NaN if trainingValues is empty or constant).

    HoldOutForecastComparison picks a forecasting method empirically: it hides the last holdOut points (at most a third of the series), fits Naive, Mean, Drift, SimpleExponentialSmoothing, HoltLinearTrend, LinearTrend, an AR model (order chosen by BIC), and, when seasonalPeriod (default 1, meaning none) is 2 or more and there is enough history, SeasonalNaive and HoltWintersAdditive, on the earlier history, and scores each against the hidden points. Scores is sorted by RMSE (best first) with each method’s forecast attached; BestMethodByRmse names the winner and Skipped explains methods that could not run (too little data, or a non-finite forecast). Requires at least 3 x holdOut and 8 points in total.

    Twenty-two general-purpose series transforms and filters: lag/lead and differencing operators, cumulative and percentage-change series, moving-average and exponential-smoothing families, rolling and expanding window statistics (a shared fourteen-statistic vocabulary: Mean, Sum, Minimum, Maximum, Median, StandardDeviation, Variance, Range, MedianAbsoluteDeviation, Skewness, Kurtosis, Count, Quantile, CoefficientOfVariation), detrending, missing-value filling, three denoising filters (median, Hampel, Savitzky-Golay), the Box-Cox power transform, a Kalman local-level filter, and a one-call series profile.

Output length matches input; the first order x lag positions are NaN. Differencing a straight line once gives a constant; twice gives zeros.

    LagSeries shifts a series so each position holds the value lag (default 1) steps earlier, or, with a negative lag, from later (a lead), to align a series with its own past or future for feature building. Positions with no source value are NaN.

    LaggedFeatureMatrix turns a series into a supervised-learning table: Features[i][j] is the value at Lags[j] steps before the target of row i, Targets[i] the series value at RowIndices[i]. Rows start once the largest lag is available. Use it to feed ordinary regression or classification with autoregressive features, for example lags 1, 2 and 7 of daily volume.

    CumulativeSeries computes a running Sum (default, Kahan-compensated for numerical stability), Product, Maximum, Minimum or Mean at each position. A NaN input makes Sum/Product/Mean NaN from that point onward, while Maximum/Minimum ignore NaN. Use the cumulative sum for a running balance, maximum for a high-water mark.

    PercentChangeSeries computes relative change over periods (default 1) steps — value / earlier value - 1, so 0.10 means up 10 percent — for growth rates of volumes or balances; for prices, prefer LogReturnSeries. NaN at the first periods positions and wherever the earlier value is 0 or NaN.

    LogReturnSeries computes the natural-log return of a strictly positive series over periods steps, which adds up over time and is approximately symmetric — the usual input for volatility and spike work. NaN wherever either value is 0, negative or missing.

    SimpleMovingAverageSeries averages a sliding window of values, with alignment (Trailing default, Centered or Leading) controlling where the window sits and minPeriods (0 default, meaning the full window) controlling how much of a partial window at the edges is tolerated. Missing values inside the window are skipped.

    WeightedMovingAverageSeries computes a weighted average of the latest values using caller-supplied weights (oldest to newest, normalised by their sum internally), so recent points can count more, for example [1, 2, 3] for a linear-weighted three-point average. Throws if the weights sum to (near) zero.

    ExponentiallyWeightedSmoothingSeries computes a series where recent observations fade in weight geometrically rather than dropping out of a hard window. Give exactly one of alpha (weight on the newest value) or span (roughly the number of periods averaged; alpha = 2/(span+1)). adjust false (default) uses the simple recursive form starting from the first value; adjust true divides by the running sum of weights, removing start-up bias.

    ExponentialMovingStatisticsSeries tracks an exponentially weighted mean and standard deviation recursively, so recent behaviour dominates — an adaptive baseline for comparing each new value with the running mean in units of the running standard deviation. Give exactly one of alpha or span; the first standard deviation is 0.

    RollingStatisticSeries computes any of the fourteen window statistics (see section intro) over a sliding window, with alignment, minPeriods and, for Quantile, the quantile argument (default 0.5). Count always reports the finite count regardless of minPeriods; statistics needing more points than are present (for example standard deviation of one value) are NaN.

    ExpandingStatisticSeries computes the same statistic vocabulary but over everything from the start of the series up to and including each position, for a running mean, running volatility, or any statistic of history accumulated so far. minPeriods (default 1) delays the first reported value. Order-based statistics (Median, MedianAbsoluteDeviation, Quantile, Skewness, Kurtosis) are capped at 20,000 values — use RollingStatisticSeries with a window for longer series.

    RollingCorrelationSeries computes the Pearson correlation between two aligned series over a trailing window (at least 3), to watch whether two metrics that normally move together have decoupled. Pairs with a missing value on either side are dropped; a position is NaN with fewer than 3 valid pairs, before the window fills, or when either side is constant within the window.

    RollingZScoreSeries computes how many standard deviations each value sits from the mean of its preceding window (at least 2), to flag values unusual relative to recent history (for example a payment far above a customer’s last 30). By default (includeCurrent false) the baseline excludes the current value, so a spike does not dilute its own baseline. NaN until minPeriods values are available, when the window’s standard deviation is 0, or when the current value itself is missing.

    DetrendSeries removes a trend so what remains is the fluctuation around it: Mean (subtracts the average), Linear (default; subtracts a fitted straight line) or Polynomial (degree 2 to 6, fitted by least squares against a time index scaled to [-1, 1]). Returns Detrended, Trend, Intercept/SlopePerStep (both NaN for Polynomial) and RSquared (the trend’s explained variance share). Requires more points than the fit has parameters.

    FillMissingValuesInSeries fills NaN/infinite gaps with LinearInterpolation (default; straight line between neighbours), ForwardFill, BackwardFill, NearestValue, SeriesMean, SeriesMedian or a supplied Constant. Leading or trailing gaps a directional rule cannot reach fall back to the nearest known value for interpolation and stay NaN for the fill methods, counted in RemainingMissing; FilledIndices lists what was actually filled. Use it to make a series finite before autocorrelation, spectral or forecasting tools, which reject missing values.

    MedianFilterSeries replaces each value with the median of a small, odd-length window (at least 3, default 3) centred on it, removing isolated spikes and dropouts while keeping genuine level shifts sharp, unlike a moving average which smears them. Edges repeat the first/last value to fill the window.

    HampelOutlierFilter flags and replaces spikes by comparing each value with the median of its halfWidth (default 3, points on each side) neighbourhood, scaled in robust standard deviations (1.4826 x median absolute deviation). A value beyond nSigmas (default 3) robust deviations is an outlier; Cleaned replaces outliers with the local median, OutlierIndices/OutlierScores (infinite if the local deviation is exactly 0) and LocalMedians describe them. Less fooled by the outliers themselves than a mean-and-standard-deviation approach would be.

    SavitzkyGolayFilter smooths a series, or estimates its first or second derivative, by fitting a low-order polynomial (polynomialOrder, default 2, below windowLength) to a sliding odd windowLength (default 5) by least squares — unlike a moving average, it preserves peaks and sharp features. samplingInterval (default 1) scales derivative units. Near the series ends, the polynomial fit to the nearest full window is evaluated at the edge position. A polynomial of degree up to polynomialOrder is reproduced exactly.

    BoxCoxTransformSeries applies the Box-Cox power transform (x^lambda - 1)/lambda (natural log at lambda = 0) to a strictly positive series, to stabilise variance and symmetrise skewed data (amounts, counts, durations) before modelling. lambda 1 is a shift, 0.5 a square root, -1 a reciprocal; left NaN (default), it is chosen automatically by maximising the Box-Cox profile log-likelihood between -3 and 3 (LambdaEstimated then true). Every value must be above 0 and finite — add an offset first if any are 0 or negative.

    KalmanLocalLevelFilter runs a Kalman filter for a local-level model (a slowly wandering hidden level plus measurement noise), given processVariance (0 or more; drift per step) and measurementVariance (above 0; observation noise). FilteredLevels/FilteredVariances are the estimate and uncertainty after each observation; OneStepPredictions, Innovations and StandardisedInnovations (values beyond about 3 are unusual) describe forecast surprises; LogLikelihood and SteadyStateGain (the limiting weight on a new observation) summarise the fit. With processVariance 0, the level is simply the running mean.

    SeriesProfile is a quick diagnostic overview to decide what to do with an unfamiliar series next: length, missing count, range, mean, spread, whether it is constant or monotonic, linear trend slope, lag-1 autocorrelation, a Mann-Kendall trend p-value, zero and negative counts, the longest run of identical values, and a suggested seasonal period (0 if none found, via EstimateSeasonalPeriodByAutocorrelation). Notes lists plain-language observations such as “contains missing values” or “strong trend is present”. Missing values are counted and excluded from the other figures; call this first, then the specific tool it points to.

    Sixteen tools for frequency-domain and signal analysis: the forward and inverse discrete Fourier transform, two power-spectrum estimators (periodogram and Welch), dominant-period extraction, a spectral shape summary, a periodicity significance test, convolution and template matching, the Hilbert envelope/phase transform, the discrete cosine transform, Haar wavelet decomposition and denoising, a frequency-domain brick-wall filter, zero-crossing analysis and peak/trough detection.

    FourierTransformOfSeries computes the full discrete Fourier transform of an evenly spaced series of any length, splitting it into sinusoids. Real/Imaginary are the unnormalised complex transform (n coefficients for n input values); the one-sided Frequencies (bins 0 to n/2, in cycles per samplingIntervalSeconds, default 1), Magnitudes, Amplitudes (the equivalent cosine wave’s amplitude) and Phases cover the physically meaningful half. Bin 0 is the mean level.

    InverseFourierTransform is the counterpart: it reconstructs a series from complex frequency coefficients supplied in the same Real/Imaginary layout FourierTransformOfSeries produces (bin 0 first, negative frequencies in the upper half), typically after editing coefficients (for example zeroing high frequencies) to get a filtered series back. Imaginary in the result is close to 0 when the input coefficients had conjugate symmetry.

    PowerSpectrumOfSeries computes the one-sided power spectrum (periodogram): the series is de-trended (detrend, default Mean, or Linear) and optionally windowed (window: None default, Hann, Hamming, Blackman, to reduce spectral leakage) before transforming. Power[k] is scaled so a cosine of amplitude A exactly on a bin has power A^2 n / 2; PeakFrequency/PeakPeriod/PeakPower identify the strongest non-zero-frequency bin and TotalPowerExcludingDc sums the rest. Cycles longer than the series length cannot be resolved.

    WelchPowerSpectrum averages periodograms of overlapping segmentLength (at least 8) segments — each de-trended, windowed (window default Hann) and transformed — for a smoother, more reliable spectrum from long, noisy series, at the cost of coarser frequency resolution (bin spacing 1/(segmentLength x interval)). overlapFraction (default 0.5) sets segment overlap and Segments reports how many were averaged.

    DominantPeriodsOfSeries finds the strongest repeating cycles from local peaks of the power spectrum (excluding the zero-frequency mean), ranked by power, up to topK (default 3). Periods/PeriodsInSamples/Frequencies locate each peak with Powers and PowerShares; a cycle that does not fit a whole number of times leaks into neighbouring bins (use a window to reduce this), and long periods are coarsely spaced by the frequency resolution. May return fewer than topK entries.

    SpectralSummary reduces a spectrum’s shape to a handful of numbers that separate noise-like, cyclic and smooth signals: SpectralEntropyBits/NormalisedSpectralEntropy (near 1 for white noise, near 0 for one pure cycle), SpectralFlatness (geometric over arithmetic mean power; 1 is perfectly flat, near 0 is peaky), CentroidFrequency (power-weighted mean frequency), RolloffFrequency (below which 85 percent of power lies), and DominantFrequency/DominantPeriod/DominantPowerShare. The zero-frequency mean is excluded and the series is mean-removed first; a series with no variation returns NaN ratios.

    FisherPeriodicityTest is Fisher’s exact g test for one hidden periodicity against a white-noise null: g is the largest periodogram ordinate divided by the sum of all ordinates (excluding the mean and any Nyquist bin). PValue is the exact probability of a g at least that large under white noise; Significant flags it below alpha (default 0.05); PeakBin/PeakPeriodInSamples locate the candidate cycle. The test targets a single strong cycle — several equal-strength cycles dilute g. Needs at least 8 values.

    LinearConvolution convolves a series with a kernel: Full (default; all n + m - 1 overlapping positions), Same (the central n values aligned with the input) or Valid (only positions of full overlap). For example [1, 2, 3] convolved with [0, 1, 0.5] gives [0, 1, 2.5, 4, 1.5] in Full mode. Use it for custom smoothing or differencing filters, or to combine two delay distributions. Work is capped at 200 million multiplications.

    TemplateMatchNormalisedCrossCorrelation slides a template pattern along a series, scoring each stretch with normalised (Pearson) correlation so level and scale are ignored (1 perfect match, 0 unrelated, -1 inverted). Use it to find repeats of a known shape, for example a structuring pattern of deposits. BestOffsets/BestScores list up to topK (default 3) non-overlapping matches at least half a template length apart scoring at least minScore (default 0.8). The template must be at least 3 values and not constant.

    HilbertEnvelopeAndPhase computes the analytic signal of an oscillating series via the Hilbert transform: Envelope (instantaneous amplitude), Phase (unwrapped radians) and InstantaneousFrequency (phase change per sample, by central differences, scaled by samplingIntervalSeconds) at every point. Use it to track how a cycle’s strength or speed changes over time. The method treats the series as periodic, so results near both ends are distorted unless it covers whole cycles, and it is only meaningful for narrow-band signals — remove mean and trend first.

    DiscreteCosineTransformSeries computes the orthonormal type-II DCT (forward, inverse false default) or its inverse (inverse true), packing a smooth series’ shape into a few low-order coefficients for compression, smoothing by discarding high-order terms, or a compact feature vector. The transform preserves the sum of squares; a forward then inverse call round-trips exactly.

    HaarWaveletDecomposition performs a multi-level Haar wavelet decomposition, splitting a series into a coarse Approximation and per-level Details at successively coarser time scales. The series length must be divisible by 2^levels (levels, default 3, 1 to 30). DetailEnergyShares shows which time scale dominates the variation; for [4, 2, 5, 5] with one level the approximation is [3, 5] and details are [1, 0].

    HaarWaveletDenoise decomposes a series with the Haar wavelet, shrinks small detail coefficients assumed to be noise, and reconstructs, preserving abrupt level changes better than a moving average would. threshold (default NaN, meaning the universal threshold sigma x sqrt(2 ln n) with sigma estimated robustly from the finest details) is applied with soft (default true; shrinks toward zero) or hard thresholding. NoiseStandardDeviation is always reported even when threshold is supplied directly, and ZeroedCoefficients of DetailCoefficients shows how much was removed.

    FrequencyDomainFilterSeries removes chosen frequencies with an ideal brick-wall cut-off: LowPass (keeps at or below lowCutoff), HighPass (at or above lowCutoff), BandPass or BandStop (between lowCutoff and highCutoff, which is required for these two). Filtered is the kept signal, Removed is the input minus Filtered, and KeptFrequencyBins of TotalFrequencyBins shows the retained share. Sharp cut-offs ring near the series ends and treat it as periodic — use SavitzkyGolayFilter instead where that matters.

    ZeroCrossingAnalysis counts and locates points where a series crosses a chosen level (default 0) by linear interpolation between neighbouring samples. Count/RatePerSample measure oscillation frequency and MeanCyclePeriodInSamples is twice the mean crossing spacing (a full cycle has two crossings; NaN with fewer than 2 crossings). Remove the mean or trend first to get crossings of the average level rather than of the raw level.

    PeakDetectionInSeries finds local peaks (a value higher than both neighbours; the middle of a flat top counts once) or, with findTroughs true, troughs, each with a Prominences value — how far it rises above the higher of the two lowest points reached before a taller peak or the series end on either side, which separates real peaks from small wiggles. Filters are minHeight (absolute level, NaN default means none), minProminence (default 0) and minDistance (default 1; of two peaks closer than this, only the taller survives). For [0, 2, 0, 3, 0, 1, 0] the peaks are at indices 1, 3 and 5 with prominences 2, 3 and 1.

    TransactionTimingTools.cs

    Fourteen tools built specifically around epoch-second event timestamps for fraud and AML timing analysis: timestamp parsing and formatting, calendar feature extraction, inter-arrival and gap statistics, bucket resampling, trailing- and multi-window velocity features, a Poisson rate-change test, burst detection, sessionisation, calendar-cycle profiling and periodicity/dispersion scoring. This class is distinct from the Velocity and Volume rule-taxonomy pattern (a TTL Counter incremented and read by Activation Rules, documented in Velocity and Volume): these are stateless array functions an analytics agent calls directly over a batch of timestamps, with no engine counter, cache or rule involved, and none of their 14 literal tool names overlaps anything in that rule-taxonomy page. All validate timestamps against the supported date range (years 1 to 9999) and throw naming the offending position if a value looks like it is in milliseconds rather than seconds.

    ParseTimestampsToEpochSeconds converts ISO 8601 timestamp strings (for example 2021-01-01T00:00:00Z, ...+01:00, or a plain 2021-01-01 10:30:00) into epoch seconds (since 1970-01-01 00:00:00 UTC, fractional seconds included), the format every other tool in this class expects. Strings with an offset or Z convert to UTC; strings with none are assumed already UTC. The call fails, naming the position, if any string cannot be parsed.

    FormatEpochSecondsAsIso is the inverse: it formats epoch seconds as ISO 8601 UTC strings (yyyy-MM-ddTHH:mm:ss.FFFFFFFZ, fractional seconds to 100 nanoseconds) so results from the timing tools can be read as dates.

    TimestampCalendarFeatures breaks epoch-second timestamps into calendar features — hour, ISO day of week (1 Monday to 7 Sunday), day of month, month, year, day of year, a weekend flag and second of day — in a chosen utcOffsetMinutes (default 0, -1080 to 1080; daylight saving is not applied, so pass the offset actually in force). Use it to build night-time or weekend-activity features for fraud and AML rules.

    InterArrivalTimeStatistics computes statistics of the gaps between successive events (sorted first, so input order does not matter): Gaps in seconds, CoefficientOfVariation (about 1 for random/Poisson arrivals, near 0 for machine-regular activity, well above 1 for bursty activity) and Burstiness ((sd-mean)/(sd+mean), from -1 perfectly regular through 0 random to +1 extremely bursty), plus EventsPerHour over the observed span. Needs at least 2 events (3 for the spread figures); duplicate timestamps give zero-length gaps.

    DetectTimeGaps finds unusually long pauses between events — dormancy, outages, missing feed data, reactivation after silence — by an absolute minGapSeconds or, left NaN (default), gapMultiple (default 5) times the median gap. Each flagged gap reports the surrounding event indices, their epoch seconds and the pause length; events must already be in ascending time order.

    ResampleEventsToBuckets aggregates an irregular event stream into fixed-length, evenly spaced bucketSeconds (default 3600) buckets — the step needed before autocorrelation, seasonality, forecasting or spectral tools can run on event data. Buckets align to originEpochSeconds (default 0, so day buckets start at UTC midnight) and run continuously from the first to the last event; includeEmptyBuckets (default true) keeps zero-count gaps so the result stays evenly spaced. Counts, and, with an optional values array, Sums/Means/Minimums/Maximums per bucket. Refuses to build more than 200,000 buckets.

    TrailingWindowVelocity computes, at every event, how many events (and how much value) occurred in the trailing windowSeconds ending at that event — “transactions in the last hour” or “amount in the last 24 hours” per transaction. The window is (t - windowSeconds, t]; includeCurrent (default true) controls whether the event itself counts in its own window. Also returns RatesPerHour and SecondsSincePreviousEvent. Events must already be in ascending order.

    MultiWindowVelocityFeatures takes a snapshot of activity at one moment (asOfEpochSeconds, default NaN meaning the last event) across several look-back windowsSeconds at once (for example 60, 3600, 86400), as a ready-made feature vector that can be evaluated at any point in the past without leaking later data (only events at or before asOf are counted). Returns Counts/Sums/RatesPerHour per window plus SecondsSinceLastEvent and TotalEventsUpToAsOf.

    WindowRateChangeTest tests whether the event rate in a recent window is unusually high or low against a preceding baseline window, under a Poisson assumption. recentSeconds and baselineSeconds define the two windows ending at asOfEpochSeconds (default last event); ExpectedRecentCount is what the baseline rate predicts (the baseline count is floored at 0.5 so a silent baseline still gives a usable expectation); PValueIncrease and PValueDecrease give the one-sided Poisson tail probabilities. Use it to detect a sudden surge or collapse in activity for a customer or channel.

    EventBurstDetection finds bursts — short periods with at least minEvents (at least 2) events inside a windowSeconds window, such as rapid-fire card attempts or structured deposits. Overlapping qualifying windows are merged into one burst; each reports its event span, EventCount, MaximumWindowCount (the busiest single window) and PValue (the chance of that many events in one window at the overall average Poisson rate; NaN if the overall span is 0). Events must be in ascending order.

    SessionizeEvents splits an event stream into sessions, starting a new one whenever the gap since the previous event exceeds timeoutSeconds (a gap exactly equal to the timeout does not start a new session). SessionIds[i] gives each event’s zero-based session; Sessions lists each session’s event span, count, start/end and duration. Use it to group transactions into visits or activity episodes.

    CalendarCycleProfile profiles when events happen within a calendar cycle — HourOfDay (default), DayOfWeek (Monday first), DayOfMonth or MonthOfYear — in a chosen utcOffsetMinutes. Labels/Counts/Shares give the distribution, PeakLabel the busiest bin, EntropyBits/NormalisedEntropy (1 means perfectly even) measure spread, and a chi-square ChiSquareStatistic/PValue tests against a uniform distribution (day-of-month uniformity is only approximate, since months differ in length). Use it to see whether activity concentrates at unusual times, for example overnight or weekends.

    EventPeriodicityScore measures how machine-like an event stream’s timing is, to catch scheduled, automated or beaconing behaviour such as identical-interval payments: MedianIntervalSeconds, CoefficientOfVariation (near 0 for clockwork regularity, about 1 for random arrivals, above 1 for bursty) and ShareWithinTolerance (the fraction of gaps within toleranceFraction, default 0.1, of the median; 1 means every gap is essentially identical). Reading summarises as highly regular (share at least 0.9 and variation below 0.2), somewhat regular, or irregular. Needs at least 3 events, sorted first.

    CountDispersionIndexTest is an index-of-dispersion test for event counts per equal-length period (for example transactions per hour, produced by ResampleEventsToBuckets): DispersionIndex (variance over mean) is near 1 for Poisson-random arrivals, well above 1 means overdispersion (bursts or clustering; small PValueOverdispersed), well below 1 means underdispersion (unnaturally regular counts; small PValueUnderdispersed). ChiSquareStatistic is (n-1) x DispersionIndex with n-1 degrees of freedom. Needs at least 3 non-negative periods with a positive mean.
