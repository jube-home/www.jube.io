    • Relationships and Regression Tool Catalogue

    Relationships and regression tool catalogue

    Per-tool index for every one of the 55 literal [AdvancedAnalyticsTool("...")] identifiers under Jube.Service/Agent/AdvancedAnalyticsTools/RelationshipsAndRegression, so that a claim naming one of these tools by its exact callable name can be checked against a real entry. Unlike the hypothesis tests family, this group had partial coverage already: LinearRegression.md walks a worked R-versus-AdvancedAnalytics course that names several of these tools with real behavioural detail, and LogisticRegression.md, the Bayesian networks score-based structure learning and parameter learning pages each name one more in passing. Those ten tools are listed below with a citation instead of being re-described; every other tool gets its own entry here. All tools live in Jube.Service.Agent.AdvancedAnalyticsTools.RelationshipsAndRegression and require the Analytics permission. Several share a common input convention: inputs is one row (array) per observation with one entry per predictor column, outputs is the matching outcome array, variableNames optionally labels the predictor columns (x1, x2, … otherwise), includeIntercept defaults to true, confidenceLevel defaults to 0.95, and dropIncompleteRows defaults to false (rejecting any row containing NaN rather than silently dropping it) — these are described once here rather than repeated in every entry below.

    Already covered elsewhere

    PearsonCorrelation and SpearmanRankCorrelation — described in LinearRegression.md, Procedure 3: the Fisher z confidence interval each adds beyond base R’s cor().

    CorrelationMatrix — described in the same procedure: every pairwise correlation with a matching p-value matrix, pair counts, and the strongest pairs pre-ranked with Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-values.

    RankPredictorsByCorrelation — described in LinearRegression.md, Procedure 4: screens every column against one target, ranked by absolute strength with a p-value and adjusted p-value per column.

    FitSimpleLinearRegression — described in LinearRegression.md, Procedure 6: the coefficient table plus R squared, adjusted R squared, the F test, AIC/AICc/BIC and Durbin-Watson that summary.lm() does not print.

    PredictWithLinearRegression — described in LinearRegression.md, Procedure 8: fits and predicts in one call, with a confidence interval for the mean response, a wider prediction interval for a single new observation, leverage, and an ExtrapolatedRows count.

    FitMultipleLinearRegression — described in LinearRegression.md, Procedure 9 (confidence interval on every coefficient at the caller’s confidenceLevel) and in ParameterLearningAndCPTs.md (a flat multiple regression as the nearest single-node analogue of a continuous Bayesian network node, with no notion of a second node’s own conditional distribution feeding into it).

    StepwiseLinearRegression — described in LinearRegression.md, Procedure 10 (Forward/Backward/Both selection by AIC, BIC or p-value entry/exit, every step recorded, with a warning that stepwise p-values are optimistic) and named again in LogisticRegression.md as OLS-only, not applicable to a binomial model.

    CompareModelsByInformationCriteria — described in ScoreBasedStructureLearning.md: AIC, its small-sample correction AICc, and BIC computed from supplied log-likelihoods and parameter counts, with Akaike and BIC weights for relative support across candidate models.

    ApplyLinkFunction — described in LogisticRegression.md: applies a link (Logit with inverse: true is the logistic function) generally for any link, reporting out-of-domain values through InvalidCount instead of silently returning NaN.

    Correlation and association (CorrelationTools)

    KendallTauCorrelation measures ordinal association as the surplus of concordant pairs over discordant pairs, scaled to -1..+1. All three variants are always returned — TauA (ignores ties), TauB (the tested default, corrects for ties) and TauC (adjusts for rectangular tables with different numbers of distinct values) — with variant choosing which one populates Tau and the confidence interval. The z statistic and p-value use the tie-corrected null variance of the concordance surplus; the interval is the Fisher-type interval on the chosen variant. Cost grows with the square of the row count, so at most 30000 pairs are accepted; ignoreMissing controls whether incomplete pairs are dropped (reporting the count) or rejected.

    PointBiserialCorrelation measures how strongly a numeric variable differs between two groups defined by a 0/1 group flag (for example amount for fraud=1 versus fraud=0) as the Pearson correlation between the flag and the numbers; its p-value is identical to the pooled two-sample t-test p-value. It returns the coefficient, t statistic, Fisher confidence interval, each group’s mean and size, and the pooled standard deviation. Both groups need at least one row and the flag must contain only 0 and 1; a constant numeric variable gives NaN.

    BiserialCorrelation estimates the correlation between a numeric variable and a 0/1 flag that is really a dichotomised normal latent variable (for example pass/fail from an underlying score), using the biserial coefficient (M1-M0)/s * p*q/y, where y is the standard normal density at the split. It is always larger in magnitude than the point-biserial coefficient and can exceed 1 in absolute value when the latent-normal assumption fails; TStatistic holds a z statistic against zero, DegreesOfFreedom is NaN, and no confidence interval is produced. Use PointBiserialCorrelation instead when the flag is a genuine two-valued measurement rather than a dichotomised continuum.

    CovarianceMatrix computes the sample covariance matrix of several numeric columns, plus column means, variances, standard deviations, the total variance (trace) and the generalised variance (determinant). Covariance[i][j] is in the product of columns i and j’s units, so it is not comparable across pairs — use CorrelationMatrix for a unit-free view. unbiased (default true) divides by n-1 rather than n; with ignoreMissing true, rows containing NaN are dropped whole (listwise) to keep the matrix positive semi-definite, otherwise they are rejected. A determinant near zero signals the columns are nearly linearly dependent.

    PartialCorrelation measures the relationship between x and y after removing the linear influence of one or more controls from both — the correlation of the two sets of regression residuals — to ask whether x and y are still related once confounders are held constant. It returns the coefficient, a t statistic with n-2-k degrees of freedom (k controls), a two-sided p-value and a Fisher interval with standard error 1/sqrt(n-3-k), alongside ZeroOrderCorrelation, the raw (uncontrolled) correlation for comparison. method is Pearson or Spearman (which ranks every variable first, a rank-based approximation); Kendall is not supported here.

    SemiPartialCorrelation measures x’s unique contribution to y: the correlation between y itself and the part of x left over after regressing x on the controls (only x’s side is residualised, unlike PartialCorrelation, which residualises both). Its square is the increase in R squared when x is added to a regression of y on the controls alone. The t statistic and p-value are those of x’s own coefficient in a regression of y on x plus the controls, with n-k-2 degrees of freedom; no confidence interval is produced (fields are NaN). method is Pearson or Spearman; Kendall is not supported.

    PartialCorrelationMatrix computes the partial correlation of every pair of columns (at least 3) controlling for every other column at once, derived from the inverse of the Pearson correlation matrix. A raw correlation that collapses once the other columns are controlled for was being driven by them. P-values use n-p degrees of freedom (p = column count). Perfectly collinear columns make the correlation matrix non-invertible and are rejected with a pointer to VarianceInflationFactors to find them first.

    MutualInformationEstimate estimates how much knowing one numeric variable reduces uncertainty about another, catching any dependence including non-monotone and non-linear shapes that correlation misses. Both variables are cut into bins (equal width by default, or EqualFrequency for skewed data; 0 chooses floor(sqrt(n/5)) clamped to 2..20 automatically) and the plug-in estimate MI = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) is returned in nats and bits, plus a normalised score MI/sqrt(H(X)H(Y)) in 0..1. GStatistic = 2n*MI (natural log) is referred to chi-square with (bins-1)(bins-1) degrees of freedom for a p-value against independence; biasCorrect (default false) subtracts the Miller-Madow correction for the estimator’s small-sample upward bias (the G test itself always uses the raw, uncorrected value). Needs at least 4 complete pairs; estimates depend on the chosen binning and are biased upward when bins are many relative to n.

    RankPredictorsByMutualInformation screens many numeric columns against one target by binned mutual information and ranks them, so non-linear and non-monotone predictors are not missed the way correlation misses them. Each column returns MI in bits, the normalised MI, the G test’s chi-square p-value and a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value across the columns, sorted strongest first with Rank 1 best; each column uses only the rows where both it and the target exist, and binning follows MutualInformationEstimate.

    CorrelationRatio measures how strongly a categorical groups code explains a numeric values variable with the correlation ratio eta (square root of between-group variance over total variance, 0 to 1) and eta squared. Unlike Pearson correlation it detects any pattern of group means, not just a linear trend across group codes. Also returned: the bias-lowered AdjustedEtaSquared (epsilon squared), the one-way ANOVA F statistic with its degrees of freedom and p-value, and each group’s label, size and mean. Needs at least 2 distinct groups and more rows than groups; a numeric variable with no variation gives NaN.

    CompareIndependentCorrelations tests whether a correlation observed in one sample differs from a correlation in a separate, independent sample (for example the same two variables in two customer segments), using the Fisher z transformation: z = (z1-z2)/sqrt(1/(n1-3)+1/(n2-3)), always two-sided, with a confidence interval for the difference in Fisher z units. Each sample size must be at least 4 and each correlation strictly between -1 and 1; it must not be used for correlations sharing a variable or measured on the same rows.

    CorrelationSummaryInterval turns a correlation already known, plus its sample size, into a significance test and confidence interval without needing the raw data: the t statistic and two-sided p-value against a true correlation of 0 (n-2 degrees of freedom), the Fisher z value and its standard error 1/sqrt(n-3), the confidence interval for the true correlation, and the smallest absolute correlation that would be significant at the complementary level — a quick read of what a given n can detect. The correlation must be strictly between -1 and 1 and n at least 4.

    Linear regression (LinearRegressionTools)

    FitWeightedLinearRegression fits a weighted least squares regression, minimising the weighted sum of squared residuals so rows with larger weights pull the line harder; use it for rows of unequal reliability (for example weight = 1/variance) or to down-weight suspect rows. Weights are treated as precision weights — they do not change the row count used for degrees of freedom — and every weight must be strictly positive. The result has the same layout as FitMultipleLinearRegression (coefficient table, R squared, F test, AIC/BIC, Durbin-Watson), all computed on the weighted scale; fitted values and residuals are reported on the original scale.

    FitPolynomialRegression fits y as a polynomial of x of the chosen degree (1 to 12) by least squares, for curved but smooth single-predictor relationships. With centreX true (the default) the terms are powers of (x - mean(x)), which avoids numerical trouble when x is large (for example a year) without affecting predictions; coefficients are then expressed in the centred variable. The result matches FitSimpleLinearRegression (coefficient table, R squared, F test, AIC/BIC, Durbin-Watson) and accepts predictAt for fitted values with mean and prediction intervals. High degrees overfit and extrapolate wildly — use SelectPolynomialDegree to choose the degree, keeping it well below the number of distinct x values.

    SelectPolynomialDegree helps choose the polynomial degree for x and y by fitting every degree from 1 up to maxDegree (2 to 12, default 6) and scoring each on R squared, adjusted R squared, residual standard error, AIC, BIC, the exact leave-one-out cross-validated RMSE (computed from the hat matrix, so no refitting is needed) and the p-value of the incremental F test against the previous degree. BestByAic, BestByBic, BestByAdjustedRSquared and BestByLeaveOneOut each name the degree that criterion prefers; BestBySequentialFTest is the degree reached by adding terms while each addition stays significant at 5 percent. When the criteria disagree, the simpler degree favoured by BIC or leave-one-out error is the safer choice. A degree that cannot be fitted (too few rows, or collinear powers) is marked Fitted: false rather than causing the whole call to fail.

    CompareNestedLinearModels tests whether a named group of predictors adds explanatory power, by comparing the full regression against a reduced regression with dropVariables removed (the partial F test) — the test for several coefficients jointly, which individual t tests in the coefficient table cannot do (for example a set of dummy variables for one categorical factor). It returns the F statistic with its degrees of freedom and p-value, the residual sum of squares, R squared, AIC and BIC of both models, and a plain-language conclusion. dropVariables must name at least one existing predictor and leave at least one predictor (or an intercept-only reduced model when an intercept is included).

    FitMultivariateLinearRegression fits the same predictors to several numeric outputs columns at once (multivariate multiple regression), returning one complete FitMultipleLinearRegression-shaped entry per outcome in Models — coefficient table, R squared, F test, AIC/BIC and Durbin-Watson for that outcome alone. The estimates are identical to fitting each outcome separately (the least squares solution does not couple the outcomes), so this is a convenience for seeing how one set of drivers affects several measures together, not a true multivariate (seemingly-unrelated-regressions) model. Outcomes are named via outputNames (y1, y2, … otherwise); rows with NaN in any outcome or predictor are rejected unless dropIncompleteRows is true.

    Generalised linear models (GeneralisedLinearModelTools)

    FitGeneralisedLinearModel fits a generalised linear model by iteratively reweighted least squares (Fisher scoring), extending regression to counts, positive skewed amounts, binary outcomes or Gaussian data in one call. family sets the outcome distribution (Gaussian, Poisson for counts, Binomial for 0/1, Gamma or InverseGaussian for positive skewed amounts) and link connects the mean to the linear predictor (Identity, Log, Logit, Probit, ComplementaryLogLog, Cauchit, Inverse); leaving link empty uses the family default (Gaussian→Identity, Poisson→Log, Binomial→Logit, Gamma and InverseGaussian→Log). The coefficient table has the estimate on the link scale, standard error, Wald z or t statistic, p-value, confidence interval, and the exponentiated estimate with its own interval (a rate ratio for Poisson/Log, an odds ratio for Binomial/Logit, a multiplicative effect for Gamma/Log). Model-level fields: deviance, null deviance, the deviance-based pseudo R squared (1 - deviance/null deviance), the Pearson dispersion (1 means the fixed-variance families fit well; well above 1 signals overdispersion), log-likelihood, AIC, BIC, a likelihood-ratio test against intercept-only, Converged and Warnings. Poisson and Binomial have dispersion fixed at 1 unless estimateDispersion is true (quasi-likelihood, scaling standard errors by the Pearson dispersion); the other families always estimate dispersion and use t statistics. An offset (for example the natural log of exposure time, for modelling a rate) is added to the linear predictor with its coefficient fixed at 1; weights supplies an optional prior weight per row. An intercept is always fitted.

    FitPoissonRegression is FitGeneralisedLinearModel fixed to the Poisson family with a Log link for a count outcome (events per customer, alerts per day, claims per policy), reporting each predictor’s effect directly as a rate ratio (ExponentiatedEstimate: 1.10 means 10 percent more events per unit increase, others held fixed). exposure (time at risk, population size, opportunity count; every value must exceed 0) adds its natural log as an offset so the model describes a rate rather than a raw count. The Pearson dispersion (chi-square over residual degrees of freedom) well above 1 signals overdispersion, meaning the standard errors are too small; refit with estimateDispersion: true (quasi-Poisson) in that case.

    FitGammaRegression is FitGeneralisedLinearModel fixed to the Gamma family for a strictly positive, right-skewed outcome whose spread grows with its mean (claim or transaction amounts, durations, costs) — the standard deviation is assumed proportional to the mean. With the default Log link, ExponentiatedEstimate is the multiplicative change in the expected outcome per unit increase; Inverse is the Gamma family’s canonical link (mean = 1/linear predictor); Identity models additive effects but can produce a non-positive fitted mean, which stops the fit. Dispersion (equal to the squared coefficient of variation) is always estimated, using t statistics. Every outcome value must be strictly positive.

    FitProbitRegression is FitGeneralisedLinearModel fixed to Binomial with a Probit link: P(y=1) = Phi(linear predictor), the standard normal distribution function. Coefficients are on the probit (z-score) scale and are roughly 0.55 times the corresponding logistic coefficients; the result carries the same coefficient table and model-level fields as FitGeneralisedLinearModel, including warnings if the classes are perfectly separated. Probit and logit rarely differ materially in fit; choose probit when a latent-normal-threshold interpretation is wanted. No held-out evaluation or calibration is performed — for a scored, evaluated logistic model use TrainLogisticRegression instead.

    FitComplementaryLogLogRegression is FitGeneralisedLinearModel fixed to Binomial with a ComplementaryLogLog link: P(y=1) = 1 - exp(-exp(linear predictor)). Unlike logit and probit the curve is asymmetric, approaching 1 more slowly than it leaves 0, which suits rare events and discrete-time hazard or “at least one event occurred” data. Coefficients are on the log-log scale; exp(coefficient) is a hazard ratio for the event over the observation window. Compare its AIC against FitProbitRegression and TrainLogisticRegression when the appropriate link is unclear.

    PredictWithGeneralisedLinearModel fits a GLM to the training rows (same family/link/weights/offset/ estimateDispersion semantics as FitGeneralisedLinearModel) and immediately predicts for newInputs. Each returned row carries the linear predictor and its standard error, the predicted mean on the outcome scale (expected count, amount or probability) and a confidence interval for that mean obtained by transforming the link-scale interval through the inverse link — which keeps probabilities within 0 and 1 and counts positive. The interval is for the mean response only, not for an individual future outcome. newOffset is required, on the same scale as offset, whenever the model was fitted with one. The model is refitted on every call; nothing is stored.

    Model diagnostics (RegressionDiagnosticsTools)

    RegressionInfluenceDiagnostics finds the rows with an outsized effect on an ordinary least squares fit, using the standard influence measures per row: Leverage (hat value, how unusual the predictor values are; average is k/n), StandardisedResidual and StudentisedResidual (the latter computed with that row left out, so an outlier cannot mask itself — beyond about 3 in size is an outlier), CooksDistance (how far every fitted value moves if the row is deleted; above 4/n is influential, above 1 severe), Dffits (change in that row’s own fitted value, in standard errors), CovarianceRatio and Dfbetas (change in each coefficient, in standard errors, if the row is deleted). Flags names which thresholds a row crosses (HighLeverage above 2k/n, Outlier at |studentised| above 3, Influential at Cook above 4/n, HighDffits above 2*sqrt(k/n), HighDfbetas above 2/sqrt(n)). By default only flagged rows appear in Rows (up to maximumRows, worst Cook first); set returnAllRows to get every row. MostInfluential always lists the top topCount rows by Cook’s distance regardless of flags. Also returned: PressStatistic (sum of squared leave-one-out prediction errors) and the leave-one-out RMSE. KeptRowIndices maps returned row positions back to the original input rows when incomplete rows were dropped.

    DurbinWatsonRegressionTest checks whether a linear regression’s residuals correlate with the residual of the row immediately before (serial correlation), computed on the rows in the order supplied — sort by time first. DurbinWatson runs 0 to 4: about 2 means no first-order autocorrelation, below about 1.5 positive autocorrelation (residuals tend to repeat sign, understating standard errors), above about 2.5 negative. FirstOrderAutocorrelation is the lag-1 residual correlation (roughly 1 - DW/2); ApproximateZ and ApproximatePValue use the large-sample normal approximation sqrt(n) times that autocorrelation rather than exact Durbin-Watson bound tables, so the p-value is only a guide above about n=30. See BreuschGodfreySerialCorrelationTest for higher-order serial correlation.

    BreuschGodfreySerialCorrelationTest tests for serial correlation of any order with the Breusch-Godfrey Lagrange multiplier test: residuals are regressed on the original predictors plus their own lags up to order (1 to 20, missing initial lags set to 0), and LM = n * R squared of that auxiliary regression is compared with chi-square at order degrees of freedom. Unlike DurbinWatsonRegressionTest it is valid with lagged outcomes as predictors and tests several lags jointly; rows must be in time order.

    BreuschPaganHeteroscedasticityTest tests whether residual spread changes with the predictors (heteroscedasticity) by regressing squared residuals on the predictors; LM = n * R squared of that auxiliary regression against chi-square with one degree of freedom per predictor. variant is Studentised (the Koenker variant, default, robust to non-normal errors) or Classical (the original Breusch-Pagan, assumes normal errors, divides the explained sum of squares by 2*sigma^4). A small p-value means ordinary standard errors are unreliable; consider an outcome transformation, weighted least squares or robust standard errors. See WhiteHeteroscedasticityTest for non-linear forms of variance change.

    WhiteHeteroscedasticityTest tests for heteroscedasticity of unknown form: squared residuals are regressed on the predictors, their squares and every pairwise product, with LM = n * R squared of that auxiliary regression against chi-square with one degree of freedom per auxiliary term. It catches variance that changes non-linearly or through interactions that BreuschPaganHeteroscedasticityTest can miss, at the cost of lower power in small samples from the larger number of auxiliary terms; it also reacts to some kinds of model misspecification more generally. At most 10 predictors are accepted, and constant, duplicated or collinear auxiliary terms (for example the square of a 0/1 dummy) are rejected with a message naming the problem.

    GoldfeldQuandtTest tests whether residual variance differs between the low and high end of one chosen predictor (sortVariable): rows are sorted by that predictor, a central fractionOmitted (0 to 0.6, default 0.2) is left out, the regression is refitted separately on the lower and upper groups, and the ratio of their residual variances (Estimate2/Estimate1, upper over lower) is compared with an F distribution. The default one-sided test (twoSided: false) only flags variance rising with the predictor; twoSided: true also flags variance falling. Omitting about 20 percent of the central rows increases power, and each group needs more rows than coefficients; the test assumes normal errors.

    ResidualNormalityTests summarises whether a linear regression’s residuals look normal, which matters for the exactness of t tests, F tests and prediction intervals in small samples. It returns the residual mean, standard deviation, skewness, excess kurtosis, the Jarque-Bera statistic and its chi-square (2 df) p-value, the Shapiro-Wilk statistic and p-value (only when 4 to 5000 residuals are available and they are not all identical, otherwise NaN), the extremes, and the fractions of residuals beyond 2 and 3 standard deviations (about 0.046 and 0.003 under normality). With many rows the tests flag trivial departures while coefficient inference stays approximately valid by the central limit theorem; with few rows heavy tails or skew matter more.

    RamseyResetTest tests for omitted curvature or non-linear terms: powers 2 up to power (2 to 5, default 3) of the fitted values are added as extra predictors, and an F test checks whether they jointly improve the fit. A small p-value means the linear specification is inadequate — curved relationship, or an omitted variable or interaction — without saying which; Estimate1/Estimate2 are the R squared of the original and augmented models. A model that already fits the outcome exactly, or whose augmented powers are collinear with the existing predictors, is rejected as undefined.

    ChowStructuralBreakTest tests whether a regression relationship changes at a known point in the row order (for example before/after a policy change): the model is fitted separately before and from breakIndex (the zero-based first row of the second period) and an F test compares the separate fits against the single pooled fit, F = ((RSS_pooled - RSS1 - RSS2)/k) / ((RSS1+RSS2)/(n-2k)). Estimate1/Estimate2 hold the two periods’ residual sums of squares; each period needs more rows than coefficients, and rows must already be ordered so the break genuinely splits the two regimes.

    VarianceInflationFactors is the main per-predictor multicollinearity check: for each column it returns VIF = 1/(1 - R squared of that column regressed on every other column), Tolerance (1/VIF), RSquaredOnOthers, StandardErrorInflation (sqrt(VIF), how many times larger that coefficient’s standard error is than with uncorrelated predictors) and a Severity label (Low below 5, Moderate 5 to 10, High above 10). A column that is an exact linear combination of the others gets VIF infinity; with a single column VIF is always 1. Outcome values are not needed — only inputs. High VIFs are fixed by dropping or combining predictors, or by switching to FitRidgeRegression.

    MulticollinearityConditionDiagnostics diagnoses collinearity with the Belsley-Kuh-Welsch method: predictor columns (plus the intercept column, when includeIntercept is true, the Belsley-recommended default) are scaled to unit length and their singular values examined. Each row of Rows is one singular value with its ConditionIndex (largest singular value divided by this one — above about 10 moderate, above 30 severe) and VarianceProportions, the share of each coefficient’s variance attributable to that component; a collinearity problem shows up as a high condition index with variance proportions above about 0.5 for two or more variables, which are then the variables involved in the near-dependency. Also returned: the overall ConditionNumber of the scaled design, the condition number and determinant of the predictor correlation matrix (near-zero determinant means near-singular), and the numerical rank. Outcome values are not needed. Use VarianceInflationFactors instead for a simpler per-variable summary.

    AddedVariablePlotData produces the data for an added-variable (partial regression) plot, showing one named variable’s effect after every other predictor has been accounted for: the outcome and the chosen predictor are each regressed on the remaining predictors, and the two residual sets are returned as paired points. By the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem, the slope of a line through those points equals the predictor’s coefficient in the full multiple regression, and their correlation is the partial correlation — useful for seeing whether a coefficient is driven by a few points or is genuinely linear. Needs at least two predictors so there is something to control for; KeptRowIndices maps the residual pairs back to the original rows.

    CheckLinearRegressionAssumptions runs the standard OLS assumption checks in one call, each reported as passed or failed at the 5 percent level with a plain-language comment: Linearity (RamseyResetTest, p-value ≥ 0.05 passes), Independence (BreuschGodfreySerialCorrelationTest order 1, plus Durbin-Watson between 1.5 and 2.5 — only meaningful when rows are in time order), Constant variance (studentised Breusch-Pagan), Normality of residuals (Jarque-Bera, and Shapiro-Wilk when available), Multicollinearity (largest VIF below 10) and Influence (no row with Cook’s distance above 1, and fewer than 5 percent of rows above 4/n). A failed check’s comment points to the specific tool to run next (RegressionInfluenceDiagnostics, VarianceInflationFactors, ResidualNormalityTests, or the individual diagnostic tool); a check that cannot be computed at all (too few rows, collinear terms) is reported as not passed, with a comment explaining why rather than failing the whole call.

    Model selection (RegressionModelSelectionTools)

    BestSubsetLinearRegression searches every combination of predictors (exhaustive, not greedy) and ranks the resulting regressions. criterion is Aic or Bic (lower better), AdjustedRSquared (higher better) or MallowsCp (lower better; a good model has Cp close to its own parameter count, computed against the full model’s residual variance). It returns TopModels overall plus BestPerSize, the single best model for each predictor count, showing how much each extra predictor actually buys. Every subset reports R squared, adjusted R squared, AIC, BIC, Cp and residual standard error. The search cost doubles with each predictor, so at most 15 predictors are accepted (maximumSubsetSize, default uncapped, can additionally restrict the largest subset tried); collinear subsets are skipped and counted rather than causing an error.

    Regularised and robust regression (RegularisedAndRobustRegressionTools)

    FitRidgeRegression fits an L2-penalised (ridge) regression, shrinking coefficients toward zero to stabilise estimates when predictors are collinear or numerous relative to the row count. Predictors are standardised to unit sample variance and the outcome centred, then the sum of squared residuals plus lambda times the sum of squared standardised coefficients is minimised; lambda 0 reproduces ordinary least squares, larger values shrink harder. It returns coefficients on both the original and standardised scale (the latter comparable across predictors), the intercept, the effective degrees of freedom excluding the intercept, R squared, the residual standard error, the generalised cross-validation score n*RSS/(n-1-EffectiveDegreesOfFreedom)^2 (lower is better, used to compare lambdas) and approximate standard errors that ignore shrinkage bias — no p-values are given, because shrunken estimates are biased. At most 500 predictor columns are accepted. Use TraceRidgeRegularisationPath to choose lambda rather than guessing it.

    TraceRidgeRegularisationPath fits ridge regression across a whole range of penalty strengths so the coefficient shrinkage can be seen and lambda chosen with evidence. For every lambda it returns the coefficients (both scales), intercept, effective degrees of freedom, R squared and the generalised cross-validation score; BestLambdaByGcv and BestIndex name the lambda with the lowest GCV, a sensible default balancing fit against complexity. Supplying lambdas uses exactly those values (up to 500); leaving it empty generates pathLength (3 to 200, default 25) values spaced geometrically from 1e-4 to 1e2 times n-1, plus lambda 0. Coefficients that change sign or swing wildly at small lambda mark collinear predictors.

    FitNonNegativeLeastSquares fits a linear model with no intercept in which every coefficient is constrained to zero or positive (Lawson-Hanson NNLS) — for mixing proportions, additive cost components, or attributing a total to non-negative drivers where a negative contribution would be meaningless. Coefficients pushed to exactly 0 are the drivers the data does not support; ActiveVariables lists those left with a positive coefficient. It returns the coefficients, residual sum of squares, RMSE and the uncentred R squared (1 - RSS/sum of squares of y, because there is no intercept to absorb the mean). No standard errors or p-values are produced, because the non-negativity constraint invalidates ordinary least-squares inference.

    FitRansacLinearRegression fits a linear regression that resists gross outliers using RANSAC: it repeatedly fits the model to a random minimal subset of rows, counts the rows within inlierThreshold of that fit, keeps the fit with the most inliers, then refits by ordinary least squares on those inliers alone. Use it when a minority of rows are corrupted (wrongly entered amounts, sensor glitches) and an ordinary fit is dragged off by them. It returns InlierModel (the full coefficient table and statistics fitted only on inliers), OrdinaryModel (the plain fit on every row, for comparison), the inlier and outlier row indices, the inlier fraction, the threshold actually used, the trials performed and the seed. Leaving inlierThreshold empty uses 2.5 times the robust scale (1.4826 x median absolute deviation) of the ordinary fit’s residuals; results are reproducible for a given seed. Because the inlier set itself was chosen by the data, the inlier model’s p-values should be treated as optimistic — the selection step is not accounted for in its inference.

    FitMultivariateRegressionByPartialLeastSquares fits a partial least squares (PLS, SIMPLS algorithm on standardised predictors) regression of one numeric outcome on many, possibly collinear, predictors by extracting components latent factors that maximise covariance with the outcome — useful when predictors outnumber or nearly equal the rows, or are highly correlated, where ordinary least squares breaks down. It returns the coefficients on the original scale and the intercept for the chosen component count, R squared, residual standard error, the share of predictor and response variance each component explains (with cumulative shares showing how many components are worth keeping), and the Variable Importance in Projection per predictor (values above about 1 mark influential predictors). With as many components as predictors the result equals ordinary least squares; fewer components is how this tool regularises.

    CrossValidateLinearRegression estimates how well a linear regression will predict unseen rows, by folds -fold cross-validation with a seeded shuffle (seed), alongside the exact leave-one-out figures computed directly from the hat matrix (no refitting needed for those). It returns per-fold RMSE, MAE and R squared, the pooled cross-validated RMSE, MAE and R squared (a large gap against the in-sample RMSE/R squared signals overfitting), the leave-one-out RMSE, the PRESS statistic (sum of squared leave-one-out prediction errors) and the predicted R squared = 1 - PRESS/total sum of squares. Every fold refits on its training rows only; folds must be between 2 and the row count, and every fold’s training set must have more rows than coefficients.

    Non-linear curves (NonlinearCurveTools)

    FitNonlinearCurve fits a named non-linear curve y = f(x) by non-linear least squares (Levenberg-Marquardt) and reports parameter estimates with inference. family selects the shape: Exponential (growth/decay, scaleexp(ratex)), AsymptoticExponential (approach to a ceiling or floor, asymptote - amplitudeexp(-ratex)), SaturatingExponential (maximum(1-exp(-ratex)), through the origin), Power (scalex^exponent, x > 0), Logarithmic (intercept + slopeln x), Logistic3 (S-curve from 0 to a maximum, with steepness and midpoint), Logistic4 (S-curve between a lower and upper level), Gompertz (asymmetric S-curve), MichaelisMenten (saturating hyperbola maximumRatex/(halfSaturation+x)), Hill (steeper saturating curve with a coefficient), GaussianPeak (bell-shaped peak) or Reciprocal (1/(intercept+slopex)); the result’s Formula field states the exact equation used. Each parameter row has the estimate, standard error, t statistic, p-value and confidence interval from the Gauss-Newton covariance sigma^2 (J’J)^-1 — an approximation that is poor in small or strongly curved samples. Model-level fields: residual sum of squares, residual standard error, R squared, adjusted R squared, Gaussian AIC and BIC, Converged and Warnings. Starting values are found automatically; supply startValues (in the order of the parameter names shown in Formula) when the automatic fit fails or lands on a poor local minimum, since non-linear fits can have several. predictAt returns predictions with delta-method intervals.

    CompareNonlinearCurveFamilies fits several named curve families to the same x and y (or, with families left empty, all twelve families that the data’s domain allows) and ranks them to find which shape fits best. For each family it returns whether it could be fitted at all (with a reason when not — for example x must be above 0 for Power and Hill), R squared, residual standard error, AIC, BIC, the Akaike weight (relative support among the fitted families, summing to 1) and the fitted parameters; Rank 1 is the lowest AIC. BestByAic and BestByBic name the winners by each criterion; a difference in AIC under about 2 means the families are practically indistinguishable, favouring the simpler or more domain-plausible one. Automatic starting values are used for every family here, so a family may fail or land on a local minimum — refit the finalists directly with FitNonlinearCurve and explicit startValues before relying on the result.
