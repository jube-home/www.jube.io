    • Model Evaluation Tool Catalogue

    Model evaluation tool catalogue

    Jube.Service.Agent.AdvancedAnalyticsTools.ModelEvaluation carries 27 tools that an AskJooby agent calls by their literal [AdvancedAnalyticsTool("...")] name. Jadek, the reply-checking agent, can only confirm a claim about a tool against this docs/ tree’s RAG index, so a tool with no entry anywhere is one Jadek can never verify. This page and its companion DataPreparationAndResampling.md document every tool in the ModelEvaluation and DataPreparationAndResampling groups for that reason. Only four files in this large directory actually carry [AdvancedAnalyticsTool(...)] methods (ModelEvaluationTools.cs, ModelSelectionTools.cs, MulticlassAndAnomalyTools.cs, MulticlassEvaluationTools.cs and RegressionEvaluationTools.cs — five files once MulticlassEvaluationTools.cs is counted separately from MulticlassAndAnomalyTools.cs); every other file in the directory (the model classes, engines, builders and math helpers) is implementation behind those tool methods and is referenced below only where it explains a tool’s real behaviour. Every tool in the group is accounted for below: documented in full, or named as already covered elsewhere with a citation, never silently omitted.

    Every tool fits, evaluates and (with the sole exception of "CrossValidateLogisticRegression", which reuses TrainLogisticRegression’s own fitting code) throws away the model it builds — nothing here persists a trained model the way the AdvancedAnalyticsTrainingRunTools family does. Rows are always split, or cross-validated, by a caller-supplied seed, so repeating a call with the same seed and data reproduces the same split and the same numbers.

    Already catalogued elsewhere

    Six tools in ModelEvaluationTools.cs and ModelSelectionTools.cs are already documented in real depth by other pages in this tree, each pairing the tool with a worked R procedure from the original training guide and tracing the exact fields its result carries:

    • "CrossValidateLogisticRegression" — Logistic Regression names it alongside "TrainLogisticRegression" as the source of the ClassificationMetrics confusion-matrix counts, precision, recall, F1, Matthews correlation and the RocAuc/RocAucLower95/RocAucUpper95 fields, and explains when to reach for it (fold stability) instead of "TrainLogisticRegression" (the coefficient table and model snapshot).
    • "EvaluateBoostedTrees" and "EvaluateDecisionTree" — Decision Trees and Boosting documents both in detail: "EvaluateDecisionTree" as the source of a confusion table, ROC AUC, average precision, log loss, Brier score, calibration, and the Rules/TreeText fields of its ModelDescription; "EvaluateBoostedTrees" as the direct match for C5-style reweighting boosting (algorithm = AdaBoost) and gradient boosting (algorithm = GradientBoosting, the default).
    • "EvaluateNaiveBayes" — Naive Bayes documents kind: Categorical as the quantile-bin, Laplace-smoothed equivalent of the guide’s own R procedure, with regularisation named as exactly the Laplace add-one count.
    • "ComputeLearningCurve" and "ComputeValidationCurve" — DecisionTreesAndBoosting.md names both as the product’s answer to “how many boosting rounds are enough” (the gbm.perf question), run against TreeCount before committing to a persisted training run.

    Everything below is a tool not covered, in full or in part, anywhere else in docs/.

    Single-family binary classifier evaluation (ModelEvaluationTools.cs)

    The four tools below share one scheme, repeated nearly verbatim in each tool’s own description: rows are split by seed into stratified train, validation and test sets, or into crossValidationFolds (2 to 10) stratified cross-validation folds whose held-out predictions are pooled. Missing-value filling and scaling are learned from training rows only; any classImbalance handling (None, BalancedWeights, Oversample or Undersample) touches training rows only; Platt probability calibration, when calibrate is true (the default), is fitted on the validation rows only; and every reported metric — ROC AUC (with a 95% interval), average precision, log loss, Brier score, a confusion table at decisionThreshold (or, left empty, the threshold that maximises validation F1), and calibration slope/intercept — comes from the untouched held-out rows. Permutation importance, when permutationRepeats is above 0 (default 3, up to 10), is the drop in held-out ROC AUC when one variable is shuffled. The shared bound across the family is at most 20,000 rows, 200 predictor columns, 500 trees and 1000 training epochs (kernel support vector machines are limited to 4000 rows), and nothing is saved. The return shape for all four is ClassifierEvaluationResult: family, scheme, seed, the settings used, row/feature/positive counts, Train/Validation/Test ClassificationMetrics, per-fold results and summary, a CalibrationSummary, a hyper-parameter Sweep table, Importance rows, a ModelDescription and Warnings.

    "EvaluateKNearestNeighbours" fits a k-nearest-neighbours classifier over a sweep of neighbourCounts (up to 16 values between 1 and 500; empty tries 1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 25, 51) and evaluates the k chosen on the validation rows only, so the test metrics stay honest even though a training ROC AUC of exactly 1 at k=1 is both expected and meaningless. Each row’s score is the share of positives among its k nearest training rows on standardised predictors, so the method needs no training step but is sensitive to irrelevant or badly scaled variables and slow on large tables. The Sweep field in the result reports the training and validation ROC AUC for every k tried.

    "EvaluateSupportVectorMachine" fits a support vector machine over a sweep of the complexity C (up to 16 values between 0.0001 and 10000; empty tries 0.1, 1, 10), with kernel set to Linear (the default, scales to 20,000 rows) or one of Gaussian, Polynomial, Sigmoid or Laplacian (curved boundaries, but limited to 4000 rows because kernel training is quadratic in row count). kernelWidth (gamma) defaults, when left empty, to 1 divided by the number of variables. Predictors are standardised on the training rows; a large C fits the training rows tightly and risks overfitting, a small C smooths. The raw margin is converted to a probability by Platt scaling on the validation rows, and C is chosen there too. The Sweep field reports training and validation ROC AUC for every C tried, and the result additionally reports the support vector count.

    "EvaluateRandomForest" fits either mode: RandomForest (the default — every split considers only a random subset of the variables, sized by featureFraction, 0 meaning the square root of the variable count) or mode: Bagging (every split sees every variable) over treeCount trees (1 to 500, default 100), each trained on a bootstrap sample sized by sampleFraction (default 1, between 0.05 and 1). The forest’s score is the average leaf probability across trees, calibrated afterwards on the validation rows; the description calls forests a robust, low-tuning default for tabular fraud and AML data with interactions, and notes that a large training-to-test ROC AUC gap is normal for forests and should not alone be read as overfitting — the test metrics are what to judge on. The result additionally reports the out-of-bag ROC AUC alongside the usual held-out metrics.

    "EvaluateNeuralNetworkClassifier" fits a small multilayer perceptron — sigmoid units trained by resilient backpropagation on standardised predictors from a seeded random start, so results are reproducible call for call — with hiddenLayers giving the unit count of each of up to 3 hidden layers of up to 64 units each (empty uses one layer of 8 units) and epochs capped at 1000 (default 100). earlyStoppingPatience (default 10, 0 to 100) halts training once the validation error stops improving for that many epochs and keeps the best weights seen; a patience of 0 trains for every epoch regardless. The description names neural networks as needing more rows than trees and being the hardest family in this catalogue to explain, recommending them specifically when smooth non-linear structure is expected and suggesting a direct comparison against "EvaluateRandomForest" and "EvaluateBoostedTrees". The result’s ModelDescription carries the training error trace alongside the usual metrics and permutation importance.

    Model and hyper-parameter selection (ModelSelectionTools.cs)

    ModelSelectionTools is constructed with an optional IAdvancedAnalyticsTrainingDataPointerReader, and every tool in this file accepts either inline inputs/labels or a cachePointer — a Guid pointing at a stored table in the Advanced Analytics training-data cache whose last column (or the column named by labelColumn) is the label — but never both at once. This is the same pointer mechanic documented for AskJooby’s chat tools at The data_pointer mechanic: a bare Guid (no tbl_ prefix) routes through IAdvancedAnalyticsCachedTableReader rather than the chat-tool cache, but the two pointer families share one read path, so a Guid minted by AdvancedAnalyticsTrainingDataPut can be handed straight to "CompareModels", "GridSearchHyperParameters" or any other tool in this file exactly as it would be handed to InspectDataSchema or PreviewDataRows.

    All nine tools recognise the same twelve EvaluationFamily values (LogisticRegression, DecisionTree, RandomForest, Bagging, AdaBoost, GradientBoosting, NaiveBayes, TreeAugmentedNaiveBayes, KNearestNeighbours, LinearSupportVector, KernelSupportVector, NeuralNetwork) and the same thirteen HyperParameter values (MaxDepth, MinLeafSize, TreeCount, FeatureFraction, SampleFraction, LearningRate, NeighbourCount, Complexity, KernelWidth, HiddenUnits, Epochs, Regularisation, BinCount), each applying only to the families it makes sense for (for example LearningRate to boosting, KernelWidth to KernelSupportVector); passing a hyper-parameter that does not apply to the chosen family throws, naming the ones that do.

    "GridSearchHyperParameters" searches every combination of one or two named hyper-parameters — parameterA with valuesA (1 to 16 values), optionally crossed with parameterB/valuesB — for one family, scored by stratified cross validation on folds (2 to 10, default 5) with fixedParameters/fixedValues held constant across every trial. The grid is capped at 64 settings (valuesA.Length * valuesB.Length); exceeding it throws, suggesting "RandomSearchHyperParameters" instead. metric (default RocAuc) ranks trials, and higher-is-better or lower-is-better is tracked per metric (RocAuc, AveragePrecision, F1, MatthewsCorrelation are higher-is-better; LogLoss and BrierScore are lower-is-better). With nested set to true (default false) it additionally runs a nested cross validation — re-choosing the best setting inside every one of outerFolds (2 to 5, default 3) outer training parts and scoring on that outer fold’s own held-out part — which gives an honest estimate of what the tuning step actually buys, reported as NestedScore alongside SelectionOptimism (the gap between the plain best score and the nested one); without nesting, the best score is explicitly flagged as optimistically biased because it is the maximum over many trials. A BestAtEdgeOfGrid warning means the chosen setting sits on the border of the searched grid and the grid should be widened. The HyperParameterSearchResult carries every trial’s mean training/held-out score, its spread across folds and its rank, the best setting, and a full ClassifierEvaluationResult for that best configuration.

    "RandomSearchHyperParameters" samples trials (2 to 64, default 20) random combinations of 1 to 4 distinct hyper-parameters from caller-given lowerBounds/upperBounds, each optionally drawn on a log scale via logScale (useful for scale parameters such as Complexity, KernelWidth, LearningRate and Regularisation, and requiring a strictly positive lower bound when enabled). Whole-number parameters are rounded after sampling. The description recommends it as the better first step whenever more than two hyper-parameters are in play, since it covers a wide space in far fewer fits than a full grid, with "GridSearchHyperParameters" reserved for refining around the winner afterwards. It shares metric, folds, nested and outerFolds with the grid-search tool and returns the same HyperParameterSearchResult shape. Trials are capped at 64 and drawn from seed, so the same seed reproduces the same trial set.

    "ComputePermutationImportance" fits one model of family (default RandomForest) and measures how much each variable matters by shuffling it in the held-out rows and recording how far the chosen metric falls — RocAuc (default), AveragePrecision or LogLoss only; any other metric throws. The drop is averaged over repeats (default 5, 1 to 10) and, when crossValidationFolds is 2 or more, over every fold too, with the standard deviation across shuffles also reported. Because it is measured on unseen data rather than read off coefficients or split counts, it reflects what actually generalises; a variable whose importance sits within its own noise of zero is listed among NegligibleVariables and is a candidate to drop, and correlated variables are noted as capable of sharing importance so each looks individually weak. Importance can even come out negative, when shuffling an already-useless variable happens by chance to help. The fitted model is trained on training rows only (with calibration on validation rows) and discarded; rows are capped at 20,000 (4000 for KernelSupportVector) and 200 columns.

    "ComputePartialDependence" fits a model of family (default GradientBoosting) and shows how its predicted probability changes as one named variable moves across its own range from the 5th to the 95th percentile, over gridPoints (2 to 50, default 20) grid points, while every other variable keeps its real per-row values — classical partial dependence. At each grid point it reports the 10th, 50th and 90th percentile of the individual rows’ predicted probabilities (the spread of individual conditional expectations, not just the mean), computed over up to 500 held-out rows. A wide band around an otherwise flat mean line signals that the variable’s effect depends on interactions with other variables. The description recommends running "ComputePermutationImportance" first and reading a partial dependence curve only for variables it flags as actually mattering, since the shape of an unimportant variable means little. variable must be one of variableNames, or the call throws.

    "CompareCalibrationMethods" fits one model of family (default RandomForest) and compares four ways of turning its raw score into a probability on held-out rows: the uncalibrated output; Platt scaling (a logistic curve, robust on small calibration sets); isotonic regression (a free-form monotone curve, more flexible but prone to overfitting a small calibration set); and a constant base-rate prediction as the no-skill reference. For each it reports log loss, Brier score, expected calibration error, ROC AUC, and the calibration slope and intercept (ideal 1 and 0 respectively — a slope below 1 reads as overconfident, above 1 as underconfident), and names the method with the lowest log loss as BestMethod. Calibration is fitted on validationFraction rows (must be above 0, since there is nothing to calibrate with otherwise) and measured on a disjoint testFraction. The description notes ROC AUC only moves for isotonic calibration, since it alone can introduce ties into the ranking that the other three methods preserve exactly.

    "CompareScoreCalibrations" runs the identical four-way calibration comparison but on existing scores rather than a model this tool fits itself — the right tool for checking a rules-engine score, a vendor risk score, or any other externally produced score before treating it as a probability. scoresAreProbabilities (default true) tells it whether the scores already sit in 0..1 or are unbounded log-odds-style scores to be passed through a sigmoid for the “uncalibrated” row. Rows are split by calibrationFraction (default 0.5, 0.1 to 0.9); between 30 and 20,000 scores are required, and each class needs at least 8 rows.

    "CompareModels" runs up to twelve classification families (default: all of them) on identical stratified cross-validation folds (2 to 10, default 5, shared across every family and seed) and ranks them by metric (default RocAuc). For each family it reports the mean held-out score with its cross-fold spread, the training-to-held-out gap, the paired fold-by-fold difference to the best family, and whether that difference is “statistically indistinguishable” from the best — defined as within one standard error of the paired difference. When two families are indistinguishable the plain-language Recommendation deliberately prefers the simplest of them, in the fixed order logistic regression, naive Bayes, a single decision tree, then forests, boosting and neural networks, on the stated grounds that a simpler model is easier to explain, cheaper to run and drifts less in production. Logistic regression is included by calling the very same implementation "TrainLogisticRegression" uses. Every family runs at its own sensible defaults on the same folds, with calibration fitted on a validation slice inside each fold; the description’s suggested workflow is to tune the winner afterwards with "GridSearchHyperParameters". Bounds are tighter than the single-family tools: at most 10,000 rows (kernel support vector machines are silently skipped above 4000), 200 columns, and 12 families. The ModelComparisonResult carries the metric and its direction, the per-family Ranking, the recommended family name and the recommendation text.

    Multiclass and anomaly evaluation (MulticlassAndAnomalyTools.cs, MulticlassEvaluationTools.cs)

    "EvaluateMulticlassClassifier" fits one of six algorithms — MultinomialLogistic (softmax regression, the default and the interpretable baseline), DecisionTree, RandomForest, NaiveBayes, KNearestNeighbours or NeuralNetwork — for an outcome with 3 to 20 classes (any whole numbers, each needing at least 5 rows) and evaluates it on held-out rows split by seed, stratified, into train/validation/test or crossValidationFolds (2 to 10). It returns accuracy alongside a MajorityClassAccuracy baseline (the accuracy of always predicting the largest class), balanced accuracy, macro/micro/weighted F1, Cohen’s kappa, multiclass Matthews correlation, log loss, Brier score, macro one-versus-rest ROC AUC, per-class precision/recall/F1/support, and the full confusion matrix (rows actual, columns predicted). The description’s explicit warning is to read the confusion matrix and per-class recall before the headline accuracy, since a dominant class can make accuracy look good while rare classes are missed entirely — compare against MajorityClassAccuracy and prefer balanced accuracy or macro F1 in that situation. Class probabilities are the model’s own raw output and are never recalibrated, unlike the binary tools’ Platt step. Algorithm-specific knobs (maxDepth/minLeafSize for trees and forests, treeCount for forests, neighbourCount for k-nearest-neighbours, hiddenLayers/epochs for the neural network) are accepted regardless of algorithm but only apply to the ones that use them. classImbalance: BalancedWeights weights rows by inverse class size where the algorithm supports row weights, and otherwise falls back to oversampling. Bounds: at most 20,000 rows, 200 predictor columns, 20 classes, 500 trees, 1000 epochs; nothing is saved.

    "EvaluateAnomalyDetector" fits one unsupervised anomaly algorithm — IsolationForest (the default and described as the robust choice: random splits isolate outliers), OneClassSupportVector (a boundary around the normal rows in a Gaussian kernel space, limited to 4000 rows), KMeansDistance (distance to the nearest of clusters cluster centres), GaussianMixtureDensity (negative log density under a mixture fit) or MahalanobisDistance (covariance-aware distance from the mean) — on rows split, stratified by label when one is given, into train/validation/test. A higher score always means more anomalous, regardless of algorithm. The expected anomaly share (contamination) is taken, in priority order, from the value explicitly given, else from the share of knownAnomalyLabels actually equal to 1, else estimated directly from the score distribution; the threshold that contamination implies is set on the validation rows and every other figure comes from the test rows. knownAnomalyLabels is entirely optional (1 marks a known anomaly, 0 a normal row) — supplying it unlocks ROC AUC, average precision, precision, recall and F1 at the chosen threshold, and needs at least 3 known anomalies to be accepted; trainOnNormalRowsOnly, when labels are given, fits the detector on the label-0 rows only, the correct setup when the normal population is already known and clean. A FlaggedShareShift warning fires when the share of test rows actually flagged differs substantially from the expected contamination. The result also returns the test score quantiles and the top-scoring test rows by original row number, which is the most direct way to see what the detector is actually flagging. Bounds: at most 20,000 rows and 200 columns (4000 rows for OneClassSupportVector), up to 500 trees, up to 32 clusters; nothing is saved.

    "EstimateAnomalyContamination" takes a plain array of anomaly scores with no labels at all and estimates what share is anomalous using four independent, purely descriptive heuristics: the share of scores above a modified z-score of 3.5 (built from the median and the median absolute deviation, so it is itself robust to the outliers it is trying to find); the share above the upper Tukey fence (Q3 + 1.5 * IQR); the largest break in the sorted scores within their top 30%; and the weight of the upper component of a two-component Gaussian mixture fit to the (log) scores. Each estimate is reported with a note explaining it, or explaining why it could not be computed. The RecommendedShare is the median of whichever estimates actually agree, clamped between 0.1% and 30%, with the threshold that share implies reported alongside it. The description is explicit that every one of the four is a heuristic: they agree when there is a genuinely separated anomalous cluster, and disagree — or return nothing at all — when the scores form one smooth continuum, in which case the stated recommendation is to choose contamination from business alert capacity instead of from the data. higherIsMoreAnomalous (default true) flips the whole calculation for detectors (such as a likelihood) where a lower score is the anomalous direction. Bounds: 20 to 20,000 finite scores; fewer than 20 triggers a SmallSample warning rather than an outright failure, and scores from "EvaluateAnomalyDetector" or from any external detector are equally valid input.

    Regression evaluation (RegressionEvaluationTools.cs)

    The five single-family tools below share the same scheme as the classification tools in ModelEvaluationTools.cs: rows split by seed into train/validation/test (or crossValidationFolds, 2 to 10), scaling and missing-value filling learned from training rows only, and every reported metric — R-squared, mean absolute error, root mean squared error, MAPE (skipping zero targets), symmetric MAPE, median and maximum absolute error, correlation, and residual mean/spread/skewness — computed on the untouched held-out rows next to a baseline that always predicts the training mean. Permutation importance, when requested, is the drop in held-out R-squared when a variable is shuffled. Shared bounds: at most 20,000 rows, 200 predictor columns, 500 trees, 1000 epochs (kernel ridge regression is limited to 4000 rows); nothing is saved. The shared return shape is RegressionEvaluationResult: family, scheme, seed, settings, row/feature counts, Train/Validation/Test/BaselineTest metrics, per-fold results, a Coefficients array (populated for the linear family), a hyper-parameter Sweep, Importance rows, a ModelDescription and Warnings.

    "EvaluateLinearRegression" fits ordinary least squares (penalty: None, the default) or a penalised variant — Ridge (shrinks coefficients, suited to collinear predictors), Lasso (drives weak coefficients exactly to zero, suited to variable selection) or ElasticNet (mixes both via l1Ratio, default 0.5: 1 is pure Lasso, 0 is pure Ridge) — sweeping regularisationValues (up to 16 values between 0 and 1,000,000, standardised; empty tries 0.01, 0.1, 1, ignored entirely when penalty is None) and picking the best on the validation rows. Coefficients are reported back on the original variable scale (the change in the target per one unit of the variable) alongside the intercept. The description frames it as the transparent baseline against which every other regression tool should be compared: if a tree or forest beats it substantially, the real relationship is non-linear or has interactions, and warns that unpenalised (None) fitting becomes unstable with collinear predictors, where Ridge is the fix.

    "EvaluateTreeRegression" fits DecisionTree (one readable regression tree, with rules and tree text in the result), RandomForest (the default — averages many deep trees on bootstrap samples) or GradientBoosting (shallow trees fitted in sequence to the remaining error, tempered by learningRate). Trees capture non-linear effects, thresholds and interactions without any scaling step, but predict step functions and cannot extrapolate past the target range actually seen in training — the description recommends comparing against "EvaluateLinearRegression" and treating a small gain as evidence the true relationship is close to linear, and explicitly flags the TrainTestGap warning as the signal that a single deep tree has overfit (forests are safer; boosting needs shallow trees and a small learning rate). pruning: ReducedError prunes a DecisionTree using the validation rows; it is ignored by the ensemble algorithms.

    "EvaluateNeighbourAndKernelRegression" fits KNearestNeighbours (the default — predicts the mean target of the neighbourCounts closest training rows on standardised predictors, swept up to 16 values between 1 and 500, empty trying 3, 5, 9, 15, 25) or KernelRidge (a smooth non-linear surface with a ridge penalty of 1 / complexity, swept via complexityValues up to 16 values between 0.0001 and 10000, empty trying 1, 10, 100, with kernel defaulting to Gaussian and kernelWidth defaulting to 1 divided by the variable count). Both families are flexible, purely local methods that cannot extrapolate beyond the training data’s range and slow down as rows grow, which is why kernel ridge here is limited to roughly half the usual kernel row bound. The chosen setting is picked on the validation rows only.

    "EvaluateNeuralNetworkRegression" fits a small multilayer perceptron — sigmoid units trained by resilient backpropagation on standardised predictors and a target rescaled into the sigmoid’s own range, from a seeded start for reproducibility — with hiddenLayers (up to 3 layers of up to 64 units, empty uses one layer of 8 units) and epochs capped at 1000 (default 200). The description repeats the same caution as the classifier version: neural networks need more rows than trees, cannot extrapolate past the training target range, and are hard to explain, and recommends comparing against "EvaluateLinearRegression" and "EvaluateTreeRegression" before adopting one. The ModelDescription carries the training error trace.

    "CompareRegressors" runs up to seven regression families (default: all of LinearRegression, DecisionTree, RandomForest, GradientBoosting, KNearestNeighbours, KernelRidge and NeuralNetwork) on identical cross-validation folds (2 to 10, default 5) and ranks them by metric — RSquared (higher is better) or MeanAbsoluteError/RootMeanSquaredError (the default)/MeanAbsolutePercentageError (lower is better). As with "CompareModels", two families whose fold-by-fold difference is within one standard error are called indistinguishable, and the Recommendation then prefers the simplest. KernelRidge is silently skipped above 2000 rows. The RegressorComparisonResult carries the metric and its direction, the training-mean BaselineScore, the per-family Ranking, and the recommended family with its recommendation text. Bounds: at most 20,000 rows and 200 predictor columns; nothing is saved.

    "ComputeRegressionPermutationImportance" is the regression counterpart of "ComputePermutationImportance": it fits one family (default RandomForest), shuffles each variable in the held-out rows, and records the resulting drop in R-squared, averaged over repeats (1 to 10, default 5) and, optionally, over crossValidationFolds. It is model-agnostic across every regression family in this catalogue and, being measured on unseen data, reflects what actually generalises rather than what a coefficient or a split count merely suggests; a variable whose importance sits near zero, or within its own standard deviation of zero, is a candidate to drop, and correlated variables are noted as capable of sharing importance so each looks weaker individually. The fitted model is training-rows-only and discarded. Bounds: at most 20,000 rows and 200 columns.

    "ComputeRegressionPartialDependence" mirrors "ComputePartialDependence" for a continuous target: it fits one family (default RandomForest) and shows how the average prediction for a named variable changes across its 5th-to-95th-percentile range over gridPoints (2 to 50, default 20) points, reporting the 10th, 50th and 90th percentile of individual predictions at each point from up to 500 held-out rows. A wide band around an otherwise flat average again signals an interaction with other variables, and the description recommends reading the curve together with the variable’s permutation importance, since the shape of an unimportant variable means little. variable must be one of variableNames, or the call throws.
